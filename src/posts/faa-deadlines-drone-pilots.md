## The Flight That Was Legal Yesterday

Picture this: you've flown the same commercial gig for two years. Same drone, same client, same careful pre-flight routine. Then one morning you launch — and unknown to you, your Part 107 currency lapsed three weeks ago, or your drone's registration quietly expired last month. Nothing *feels* different. The drone flies fine. But on paper, that flight just became non-compliant, and if anything goes wrong — an incident, an insurance claim, an FAA inquiry — you're suddenly operating outside the rules you thought you were following.

This is the part of being a drone pilot almost nobody talks about. We obsess over the *flight* — weather, airspace, LAANC, line of sight. But there's a whole second layer of compliance that has nothing to do with any individual flight and everything to do with **the calendar.** Certain credentials expire on their own, on a fixed clock, and here's the catch that gets good pilots: **the FAA does not send you a reminder.** No email, no text, no push notification. The responsibility to track these dates is entirely yours, and a lapse you never noticed is still a lapse.

This post is the maintenance manual for your *paperwork* — the deadlines that expire without warning, why they're so easy to miss, and a dead-simple system to make sure one never catches you off guard.

## Why These Slip Through the Cracks

Before the specific dates, understand *why* this category of mistake is so common, because the reason tells you how to defend against it:

- **They're invisible day-to-day.** A lapsed registration doesn't change how your drone flies. There's no warning light, no error message. Everything works exactly the same right up until it matters.
- **The intervals are long.** Two years, three years — long enough that you set it and completely forget it. You renew, breathe a sigh of relief, and the next deadline is so far away it falls off your radar entirely.
- **Nobody reminds you.** Your car registration sends a notice. Your passport doesn't ping you, but you only use it occasionally so you check. Your drone credentials are the worst of both worlds: long intervals *and* no reminders *and* you use them constantly, so you assume "I'm an active pilot, I must be current." Activity is not currency.

The fix, as you'll see, is simply to make the invisible visible — to put these dates somewhere you'll actually see them *before* they pass.

## The Deadlines That Actually Expire

Here are the recurring drone-pilot deadlines that run on a clock. Exact requirements can change, so always confirm current rules at the official FAA sources — but as a rule of thumb, these are the ones that lapse on you.

### Part 107 Currency — every 24 calendar months

If you hold a Part 107 remote pilot certificate, the certificate itself doesn't disappear — but to keep *exercising* its privileges, you have to stay current. Since 2021, that no longer means re-taking the in-person knowledge test. Instead, you complete **free online recurrent training every 24 calendar months.** Miss it, and you're not legally allowed to operate under Part 107 until you do — even though your plastic certificate still looks valid in your wallet.

This is the single most-missed one, precisely because the certificate doesn't visibly expire. Pilots think "I passed Part 107, I'm good forever." You're not — you're good for 24 months at a time, and the clock is always running.

### Drone Registration — every 3 years

Most drones have to be registered with the FAA, and that **registration is valid for three years**, after which it must be renewed (a small fee, done online). This applies whether you fly recreationally or under Part 107. Three years is a long time — long enough that the renewal date is completely out of mind by the time it arrives — and an expired registration means you're flying an unregistered aircraft, which is its own violation. Don't forget the companion rule: your registration number must be **marked on the exterior** of the aircraft.

### TRUST — one-time, but keep the proof

If you fly recreationally under the Exception for Limited Recreational Operations, you're required to pass **TRUST** (The Recreational UAS Safety Test). The good news: it's a **one-time** test with no expiration. The catch: you must **keep your completion certificate** and be able to produce it if asked. It doesn't expire, but a lost certificate you can't show is a practical problem — save a copy somewhere you won't lose it.

### Remote ID Compliance — ongoing

Remote ID isn't a date on a calendar so much as a standing requirement: most drones that require registration must also meet **Remote ID** rules, either by broadcasting via a Standard Remote ID drone or by attaching a broadcast module. It's not something that "expires," but it *is* something to verify is actually working on each aircraft — a module with a dead battery or a drone with outdated firmware can quietly put you out of compliance.

### The Non-FAA Deadlines That Bite Just as Hard

Two more that aren't FAA rules but will absolutely ruin a commercial pilot's day if they lapse:

- **Insurance.** If you carry liability insurance for paid work, that policy and your certificate of insurance (COI) have an expiry date. A lapsed COI doesn't just risk a claim — many clients won't let you on site without current proof, so an expired policy can cost you the job before you ever launch.
- **Aircraft firmware & maintenance.** Not a legal deadline, but outdated firmware can affect Remote ID compliance, geofencing, and flight safety. Worth tracking alongside the legal dates.

## The System: Make the Invisible Visible

You don't need anything fancy — you need these dates to live somewhere you'll see them *before* they pass, not after. The principle is simple: **a deadline you have to remember is a deadline you'll eventually miss; a deadline that reminds you is one you'll always make.**

A workable system has three parts:

1. **Write every date down in one place.** Part 107 currency due date, registration renewal date, insurance/COI expiry, TRUST certificate location. One list. The act of gathering them in one place is half the battle — most pilots have never actually seen all their deadlines side by side.
2. **Set reminders that fire early.** Not the day of — the standard in aviation is a **90 / 30 / 7-day** cascade. Ninety days out gives you time to schedule the recurrent training or renew without scrambling; thirty days is the "do it now"; seven days is the "do not fly until this is done." Early reminders turn a deadline from an emergency into a chore.
3. **Check it on your no-fly days.** The best time to handle paperwork is exactly when you *can't* fly — a rainy, gusty, grounded afternoon is the perfect moment to knock out a recurrent course or a registration renewal. Tie the habit to the downtime you already have.

## The Bottom Line

Staying compliant as a drone pilot isn't only about each flight — it's about the credentials that expire on a clock while you're not looking. **Part 107 currency lapses every 24 months. Registration expires every 3 years. Insurance has its own date. And the FAA reminds you of none of it.** A lapse you never noticed is still a lapse, and it turns a normal flight into an illegal one without anything looking different. Write every date down, set reminders that fire 90 / 30 / 7 days out, and handle the renewals on the no-fly days you already have. Do that, and the calendar stops being a trap.
