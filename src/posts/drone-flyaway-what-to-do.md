## What a "flyaway" actually is

Pilots use the word for anything where the drone stops going where they point it. In practice that covers a few very different situations, and knowing which one you're in changes what you should do:

- **It's drifting and ignoring small inputs.** Usually lost GPS — the drone has dropped into attitude mode and is being pushed by the wind.
- **It's flying a steady line on its own.** Often a return-to-home triggered by lost signal or a low battery — it's doing what it was told, just not what you expected.
- **It won't respond at all.** Lost link with no failsafe engaged, or an aircraft fault.
- **It's being blown away.** Wind at altitude is stronger than the drone can fight, so it's losing ground even at full stick.

## What causes flyaways

Most flyaways aren't mysterious. They trace back to a handful of causes, nearly all of which were present before takeoff:

- **Compass interference.** Launching off a car roof, a steel deck, rebar-reinforced concrete, or next to a metal structure can corrupt the compass reading. The drone thinks north is somewhere else and flies accordingly.
- **Weak or lost GPS.** Taking off before the drone has a solid satellite lock — or before it has recorded a home point — means there's nothing reliable to hold position against or return to.
- **Geomagnetic activity.** Strong solar storms, measured by the **Kp index**, degrade GPS accuracy. A high-Kp day can turn a rock-steady hover into a slow wander.
- **Wind at altitude.** Ground-level wind can feel calm while it's blowing twice as hard 300 feet up. The drone climbs into it, can't hold position, and starts losing the fight.
- **A return-to-home altitude that's too low.** The drone loses signal, turns for home at 100 feet, and flies straight into the tree line between you.
- **Low battery.** An automatic low-battery landing or return can look exactly like a flyaway when you weren't expecting it.

## The first thirty seconds

When it happens, the instinct is to jam the sticks. Slow down and work the list:

1. **Stop and assess which kind it is.** Is it drifting, flying a line, or not responding? Watch it for two or three seconds before reacting.
2. **Trigger Return-to-Home.** If there's any link at all, RTH is the fastest way to get the aircraft pointed back at you.
3. **If it's drifting, fly it manually.** A drone without GPS will hold altitude but not position. It still responds — you just have to actively correct for the wind yourself. Small, deliberate inputs.
4. **Fight the wind low, not high.** If it's being blown downwind, descend. Wind is usually weaker closer to the ground, and a lower drone can often make headway it couldn't at altitude.
5. **Improve your signal.** Face the drone, orient the controller antennas the way your manufacturer specifies, and move to open ground if you can do it safely. Buildings, trees, and even your own body block signal.
6. **Note where you last saw it and what time it was.** If you lose it, those two facts are what you'll search with.

**If it's heading toward an airport or manned aircraft, that stops being an equipment problem and becomes a safety one** — contact the nearest airport or air traffic control facility and tell them.

## If it's already gone

- **Check your app's last known location.** Most manufacturers' apps record the aircraft's final GPS position, and many have a "find my drone" feature built on it. Start there, not with a wide search.
- **Pull the flight log.** The last recorded coordinates, altitude, and heading tell you where it was going and roughly how far it could have gotten on the battery it had left.
- **Search where the log points, not where you hope.** Walk the last heading. Look up — drones end up in trees far more often than on the ground.
- **If it came down on private property, knock and ask.** Don't climb fences.
- **If it hit anyone or damaged property, the FAA reporting rules may apply.** Damage over $500 to property other than your drone, or a serious injury, triggers a report within 10 days — we covered exactly when in our post on reporting a drone crash.
- **Check your protection plan.** Some manufacturer care plans cover flyaways. Many require the flight log, so don't delete anything.

## Preventing most of them before takeoff

The honest truth is that most flyaways are decided on the ground:

- **Calibrate the compass when the app asks**, and whenever you fly in a new area far from home.
- **Launch from open ground**, away from vehicles, metal decks, and reinforced concrete.
- **Wait for a strong GPS lock and a recorded home point** before you leave the ground. Every time.
- **Set your return-to-home altitude above the tallest obstacle** around your flight area.
- **Check wind at altitude, not just at your feet.** What you feel standing there is not what the drone will fight at 300 feet.
- **Check the Kp index** before a flight that depends on precise positioning.
- **Update firmware at home, the day before** — never at the field.
- **Keep it within visual line of sight.** It's the rule, and it's also the difference between watching a drift start and discovering it too late.

## Bottom line

A flyaway is rarely random. Stay calm, figure out whether you've lost GPS, signal, or the fight against the wind, trigger return-to-home, and fly it manually and low if you have to. Note where and when you lost it. Then fix the cause on the ground: calibrate, launch clear of metal, wait for GPS, set a safe RTH altitude, and check the wind aloft before you ever take off.
