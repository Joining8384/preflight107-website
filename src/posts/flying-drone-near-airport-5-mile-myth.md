## The Myth That Won't Die

Search "can I fly my drone near an airport" and you'll get the same confident answer over and over: *stay 5 miles away, and call the airport first.* It's on forums, in YouTube comments, repeated by well-meaning pilots at the field.

It's also **outdated and wrong.** There is no 5-mile rule in the current regulations, and under Part 107 you are not required to phone the tower before you fly. Pilots who cling to the myth end up in two bad spots: either they skip perfectly legal flights they were allowed to make, or — worse — they *think* being "more than 5 miles away" makes them legal when it doesn't. Let's replace the myth with what actually applies.

## Where the "5 Miles" Came From

The rule wasn't invented out of thin air — it's a fossil. Under the old recreational-flyer rules (the pre-2018 hobbyist guidance), model aircraft operators were told to **notify the airport and control tower** when flying within 5 miles of an airport. That's the origin of the number.

That framework is **gone.** It was replaced by a system built around airspace and instant digital authorization. But the "5 miles / call the airport" phrasing was simple and sticky, so it outlived the rule it came from. Today it's the drone world's equivalent of an old wives' tale.

## What the Rule Actually Is (Part 107)

Here's the key shift in thinking: **it's not about your distance from the airport. It's about the class of airspace you're in.**

Airports come wrapped in different kinds of airspace, and that's what determines what you need:

### Controlled airspace → you need authorization

Busy airports sit inside **controlled airspace** — Class B, C, D, or the surface area of Class E. To fly a drone there, you need **air traffic control authorization**, and you get it (usually in seconds) through **LAANC** — the Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability — right from a drone app, or via the FAA's DroneZone for larger requests.

LAANC also tells you the **maximum altitude** you're approved for in that spot (the "grid ceilings" you've probably seen as colored boxes on a drone map) — sometimes 400 feet, sometimes 100, sometimes 0, depending on how close you are to the runways.

### Uncontrolled airspace → no authorization needed

Most small, untowered airports sit in **uncontrolled (Class G) airspace.** Here's the part that surprises people: you can legally fly near one of these airports **with no authorization and no phone call at all** — even if you're well within that mythical 5 miles. Class G is open for Part 107 operations up to 400 feet.

*But* — and this matters — you still have to **not interfere with airport operations** and you must **yield the right of way to any manned aircraft.** So you fly with your head up: stay clear of approach and departure paths, keep visual watch for traffic, and don't set up directly off the end of a runway just because the letter of the law allows it. Legal and smart aren't always the same thing.

## "Do I Have to Call the Airport?"

Under Part 107: **no.** There is no requirement to contact the airport or tower before flying. LAANC *is* the coordination with ATC — that's the whole point of it. When your authorization comes back approved, you're cleared; you don't also need to make a phone call.

(Being neighborly is another matter. Near a small untowered field with active traffic, a courtesy heads-up to an FBO or flight school is good manners and good safety — but it's a choice, not a regulation.)

## How to Actually Check Before You Fly

Forget measuring miles. The real question is: *what airspace am I standing in, and what's my ceiling?* Two ways to find out:

1. **A LAANC-enabled app or airspace map.** It shows you the airspace class at your exact spot and the approved altitude grid, and lets you request authorization on the spot if you're in controlled airspace. This is the fast, modern answer — seconds, not phone calls.
2. **A sectional chart / the FAA's UAS Facility Maps.** These show the airspace boundaries and the pre-set altitude ceilings around airports. Useful for understanding *why* a spot is what it is.

The habit that keeps you legal: check the **airspace at your launch point** every time, not your distance from the nearest runway.

## A Quick Word for Recreational Flyers

If you're flying recreationally rather than under Part 107, the airspace logic is the same: **controlled airspace requires LAANC authorization** (the old "notify the airport" step was replaced by LAANC here too), and you'll also need to have passed **TRUST**, the free recreational safety test. The 5-mile phone call is gone for you as well.

The 5-mile guessing game gets replaced with a straight answer.

## The Bottom Line

There is **no 5-mile rule**, and under Part 107 you **don't call the airport.** What matters is the **airspace class** at your launch point: controlled airspace needs LAANC authorization (with an altitude ceiling), while uncontrolled airspace near a small airport is open — as long as you stay clear of traffic and yield to manned aircraft. Check the airspace, not the distance, and you'll fly both legal and safe.
