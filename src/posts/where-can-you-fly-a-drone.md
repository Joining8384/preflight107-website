## The Spot That Checks Out — Until It Doesn't

You've done everything right. You pulled up the airspace, and it's clean: uncontrolled Class G, no controlled-airspace rings, no active TFRs, no LAANC grid to worry about. Weather's calm, the light is good. By every airspace check you know how to run, this spot is a green light.

So you drive out, set up — and a park ranger walks over and tells you drones aren't allowed here. Or you realize the field you're standing on is inside a national park boundary. Or a city ordinance you'd never heard of says you can't launch from this public park. The airspace was perfectly legal. The *place* was not.

This is the gap that catches good, careful pilots: **the FAA controls the airspace, but the FAA does not control every "where."** There's a second, completely separate layer of rules — national parks, security-sensitive sites, and a giant patchwork of state and local laws — that governs many of the specific locations you'd actually want to fly from, and **almost none of it appears on an airspace map.** You can be 100% legal in the air and still be breaking a rule on the ground.

This post is the full map of where you can and can't fly: the places that are flat-out off-limits, the ones that need permission, the temporary closures, the local-law trap, and how to know the whole picture before you load the truck.

## First, the Mental Model: Two Separate Layers

Almost all the confusion about "where can I fly" comes from not realizing there are two independent layers of rules, and you have to clear **both**:

1. **The airspace layer (FAA).** This is what your airspace app shows you: controlled vs. uncontrolled airspace, LAANC ceilings, restricted and prohibited areas, TFRs, and security airspace. The FAA owns the air from the ground up, and these rules are about *the sky*.
2. **The location layer (everyone else).** This is who controls the *ground* you launch from and the specific place you're operating over: the National Park Service, the military, state legislatures, county and city governments, and private property owners. These rules are about *the place*.

The FAA is explicit that it doesn't regulate things like trespass, privacy, or where a city lets you take off — those are left to other authorities. So a "clear" airspace check answers layer one. It does not answer layer two. The places below are mostly layer-two problems — which is exactly why they surprise pilots who only ever check airspace.

## The Places That Are Flat-Out Off-Limits

### National Parks

This is the single most common one pilots get wrong. **Launching, landing, or operating a drone from within a National Park is prohibited.** The National Park Service banned it across the National Park System back in 2014, and the ban is about the *ground operation* — you can't take off or land inside park boundaries. That iconic shot over the canyon, the waterfall, the desert arch? Off-limits from inside the park, and rangers enforce it with real fines.

Note the trap: this is an **NPS rule, not an FAA airspace rule**, so the airspace over a national park can look perfectly open on your map. The restriction is on the land, not (necessarily) the sky. National Parks are different from National Forests and Bureau of Land Management land, which are often more permissive — but "it's public land" is not a safe assumption. Always check the specific managing agency.

### Washington, D.C.

The entire Washington, D.C. area is the most restricted airspace in the country for drones. Within roughly a **15-mile radius** of Reagan National Airport — the inner ring of the DC Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA) — drone flight is effectively **prohibited** for hobbyists and Part 107 pilots alike (and an even smaller, stricter Flight Restricted Zone sits inside that). From about **15 to 30 miles** out, the outer SFRA does allow flying, but under real restrictions you have to meet. "No Drone Zone" is the FAA's official framing for the DC core, and it's not a place to test the limits.

### Prohibited and Restricted Areas

Some airspace is permanently closed or controlled for national security and military reasons — these appear on charts as **Prohibited Areas** (think certain government and military sites, where flight is simply not allowed) and **Restricted Areas** (active military operations like live-fire ranges, dangerous when hot). These *do* show up on a good airspace map, and you treat them as hard no-go.

### Military Bases and Security-Sensitive Sites

Beyond charted restricted airspace, the FAA has established specific **airspace restrictions over many military bases and national-security-sensitive facilities** (certain Department of Defense sites, some federal facilities). These are designated no-drone zones. Don't fly over or near a military installation.

## The Places That Need Permission (Not a Ban — a Process)

### Controlled Airspace Near Airports

If your spot falls in controlled airspace — the rings around airports (Class B, C, D, or surface-level Class E) — you're not banned, but you **need authorization before you fly**, almost always through LAANC, which can be instant. The key point for "where can I fly": being *near an airport* doesn't mean you can never fly there; it means you need to be cleared into that airspace first, up to the published ceiling. (This is the airspace layer, and it's the one your map handles best. For the full breakdown, see our guides on [airspace classes](/blog/controlled-vs-uncontrolled-airspace-drone-pilots) and [LAANC](/blog/what-is-laanc-drone-pilots).)

The mistake to avoid is the opposite one: assuming that because you're *not* near a big airport, there's no airspace concern. Small non-towered airports and heliports create their own considerations, and surface-level Class E around some airports requires authorization even with no tower in sight.

## The Places That Are Closed Only Sometimes

### Stadiums and Big Events

There's a standing federal flight restriction (commonly called the "stadium TFR," though it's actually a permanent statutory rule) over any stadium seating 30,000+ during major sporting events — MLB, NFL, NCAA Division I football, and major motor speedway events — covering a 3-nautical-mile radius, surface to 3,000 feet, from an hour before to an hour after. So a field near a stadium is legal at noon and illegal by game time.

### Disasters, VIP Movements, and Wildfires

Temporary Flight Restrictions also pop up over wildfires, disaster-response areas, and wherever the President or other protected officials travel. These are invisible until you check, and they can appear with little notice. A spot that was clear yesterday can be inside a fresh TFR today — which is the whole danger of "temporary." (We go deep on this in our [TFR guide](/blog/what-is-a-tfr-drone-pilots).)

## The Trap Almost Nobody Checks: State and Local Laws

Here's the layer that catches even experienced pilots, because it's invisible to every airspace tool: **state and local governments can't regulate the airspace, but they can and do regulate where you take off and land, where you operate, and how you handle privacy.**

What this looks like in the real world:

- **City and county ordinances** that ban launching or landing a drone from public parks, beaches, or other municipal property — even when the airspace above is wide open. A lot of cities have quietly passed these.
- **State laws on critical infrastructure** — many states prohibit flying over or near power plants, refineries, water treatment facilities, prisons, and similar sites, layered on top of any federal restriction.
- **Privacy, harassment, and "peeping" laws** that apply to what you do with the camera regardless of where the drone is.
- **Trespass and nuisance laws** that govern the ground you stand on and, in some interpretations, very low flights over private property.

The catch is that this is a genuine patchwork — it varies city by city and state by state, there's no single national database that's reliably complete, and an airspace app cannot tell you that *this particular county* bans launching from *this particular park*. The only fix is local awareness: check the rules for the specific jurisdiction you're flying in, especially on public land managed by a city, county, or state.

## Private Property and the Gray Zone

Two separate questions get tangled here, so keep them apart:

- **Where you launch and stand.** You need permission to be on private property. You can't trespass onto someone's land to take off — that's a straightforward ground-level rule, and "I was just flying" isn't a defense for standing where you're not allowed.
- **What you fly over.** This is the genuinely unsettled part. The FAA owns the navigable airspace, but how that squares with a property owner's rights at low altitude is an area courts are still working out, and state nuisance, trespass, and privacy laws fill the gap. The practical takeaway: flying low over someone's private property, especially with a camera, invites both legal and human conflict even if the airspace is technically open. Discretion and permission go a long way.

## So Where CAN You Fly?

After all the restrictions, the answer is still: **a lot of places.** The clear path is:

- **Uncontrolled (Class G) airspace**, away from airports, with no TFRs — the open, low-altitude air where most legal flying happens.
- **Your own property**, or private property where you have the owner's permission.
- **Public land that allows it** — many National Forests, BLM lands, and local parks permit drones; some require nothing, some have rules. Confirm with the managing agency, don't assume.
- **Controlled airspace once you have authorization** (LAANC) up to the approved ceiling.

In all of those, you still follow the rest of the rules — 400 feet AGL, visual line of sight, no flying over people who aren't part of the operation, Remote ID, and the rest of Part 107 or the recreational guidelines.

## The Bottom Line

A green airspace check is necessary, but it is not the whole answer. The FAA owns the sky, but national parks, Washington DC, military and security sites, stadiums during games, fresh TFRs, and an enormous patchwork of state and local laws all control specific *places* — and most of them never show up on an airspace map. Run both layers every time: the airspace (which a good app makes fast) and the location (which only you can confirm for the jurisdiction you're standing in). Do that, and "where can I fly" stops being a guess.
