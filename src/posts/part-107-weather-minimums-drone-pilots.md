## The Weather Rule That Isn't About Wind

Ask a room full of drone pilots what "weather minimums" means and most will start talking about wind. Wind is important — but here's the thing: **the FAA doesn't set a wind limit for Part 107.** There's no number in the regulation that says "no flying above 20 mph." Wind is a judgment call based on your aircraft.

What the FAA *does* set — in black-and-white, non-negotiable regulation — are minimums for **visibility** and **distance from clouds**. These are the weather rules that can actually get you a violation, and they're the ones pilots most often break without realizing it, because the sky can look perfectly flyable while you're technically illegal.

If you fly commercially under Part 107, these numbers aren't suggestions. They're operating limitations, the same as the 400-foot ceiling. Let's make sure you know them cold.

## Why the FAA Set Hard Weather Minimums

Every operating rule in Part 107 traces back to one goal: keeping your drone from hitting something — a person, a building, or (the big one) a manned aircraft.

Visibility and cloud clearance are both about **see-and-avoid**. As a remote pilot you're required to keep your aircraft within visual line of sight and to yield right-of-way to crewed aircraft. You can't yield to a helicopter you can't see. When visibility drops or you fly up near a cloud base, two things happen: you lose your ability to spot traffic, and a manned aircraft descending out of that cloud has no time to spot *you*. The minimums build in a buffer so both of you have a fighting chance to see and avoid.

That's why "it looks fine" isn't the standard. The rule exists precisely for the days that look *almost* fine.

## The Two Numbers You Must Know

Under **14 CFR § 107.51**, every small-UAS operation has to meet these weather minimums, measured **from your control station** (where you're standing, not where the drone is):

### 1. Visibility: at least 3 statute miles

You need a minimum flight visibility of **3 statute miles**. That means from where you're standing, you must be able to see for at least three miles. Haze, fog, smoke, heavy rain, or blowing dust that drops you below three miles of visibility grounds you — full stop.

Here's the catch that trips people up: **statute miles, not nautical miles**, and it's *flight visibility* as seen from your position, not whatever the nearest airport reports. Airport visibility (the "vis" in a METAR) is a great starting point, but micro-conditions where you're standing can be worse. A METAR reading 10 miles at an airport 15 miles away doesn't help you if you're socked in by a marine layer at the coast.

### 2. Cloud clearance: 500 feet below, 2,000 feet horizontal

You must stay:

- **At least 500 feet below** any cloud, and
- **At least 2,000 feet horizontally** away from any cloud.

This is the one pilots blow past constantly, because on an overcast day the clouds don't feel like they're in the way — they're above you. But if the ceiling is low, that 500-foot buffer eats into your legal altitude fast. If the cloud base is at 800 feet AGL, your legal ceiling isn't 400 feet — it's 300 feet, because you have to stay 500 below the deck. And if a puffy cumulus cloud is drifting past a few hundred feet to your side, that 2,000-foot horizontal rule can quietly put you in violation even though nothing is above you.

Cloud clearance is a *3-D* rule. It's not just "don't fly into a cloud" — it's a bubble you have to stay out of.

## What About Daylight? (This Rule Changed)

For years, Part 107 was daylight-only unless you held a waiver. That changed with the 2021 rule update. **You can now fly at night under Part 107 without a waiver** — as long as your aircraft has anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles and you've completed the updated recurrent training. Civil twilight is included, same lighting requirement.

So "daylight" is no longer a blanket weather minimum. But the visibility and cloud-clearance rules above still apply, day or night — and at night, spotting clouds and gauging visibility gets genuinely harder, so those minimums deserve *more* respect after dark, not less. ## Temperature, Density Altitude, and the Minimums the FAA *Doesn't* Set

Notice what's missing from § 107.51: there's no rule about temperature, wind, or density altitude. That's not because they don't matter — it's because the FAA leaves those to your judgment as pilot in command.

That's the trap. Pilots read the regulation, see no wind or temperature limit, and assume those factors are "unregulated" and therefore fine. But your responsibility as PIC is broader than the printed minimums: you're required to determine that the operation can be conducted safely, period. A 25 mph gust that overpowers your drone, or a hot-and-high day where density altitude robs your climb power, will end your flight just as surely as a cloud will — the FAA just trusts you to make that call. The printed minimums are the floor, not the whole picture.

## How to Actually Check Your Minimums Before You Fly

Knowing the numbers is step one. Confirming them on-site is step two, and it's where good pilots build a habit:

1. **Pull the nearest METAR** for a baseline on visibility and cloud ceiling. Remember it's a *starting point* — the reporting station may be miles away.
2. **Look up, then look out.** Physically estimate the cloud base and your visibility from where you're standing. Pick a landmark you know the distance to. If you can't see three miles, you're done.
3. **Do the ceiling math.** Cloud base minus 500 feet is your real legal ceiling. If that's below the altitude your job needs, don't launch.
4. **Watch drifting clouds.** On a partly cloudy day, a single cumulus cloud tracking past can breach the 2,000-foot horizontal rule. Keep your bubble clear.

This is exactly the kind of check that's easy to skip when you're in a hurry — which is why building it into a repeatable pre-flight routine matters more than memorizing the numbers.

## The Bottom Line

Part 107's real weather minimums are simple to memorize and easy to break: **3 statute miles of visibility, 500 feet below the clouds, and 2,000 feet horizontally from them** — measured from where you stand. Wind and temperature aren't in the regulation, but they're still your responsibility. Know the numbers, confirm them on-site every time, and treat "it looks fine" as the exact condition the rule was written for.
