## What Is a Sectional Chart?

A **sectional aeronautical chart** is the standard visual navigation map for low-altitude aviation in the United States. Published by the FAA and updated every 56 days, sectionals cover the entire country in overlapping regional sections — hence the name. They pack an enormous amount of information into a single image: airports, airspace classes, terrain elevation, obstacles, restricted areas, and more.

As a drone pilot, you might wonder why you need to care. After all, you're not navigating between cities in a Cessna. But the airspace rules that govern your drone operations — particularly where you can and cannot fly without authorization — are defined by the same airspace shown on sectional charts. Understanding how to read one gives you a direct view into the regulatory layer above your head.

## The Color-Coded Airspace System

The most important thing sectional charts communicate is **airspace classification**. Each class is rendered in a distinct color and boundary style:

**Class B** — solid blue concentric circles, like a bullseye. This surrounds the country's busiest airports (think LAX, JFK, O'Hare). It typically extends from the surface up to 10,000 feet MSL and is segmented into layers with different floor altitudes. Drone operations here require LAANC authorization or a Part 107 waiver — automatic approval is rarely available.

**Class C** — solid magenta concentric circles, similar structure to Class B but smaller. Mid-sized airports like Midway or Burbank. Extends from the surface to 4,000 feet AGL in most configurations. LAANC typically applies; check the UAS Facility Map for your specific grid ceiling.

**Class D** — dashed blue circles. Airports with an operating control tower but smaller traffic volumes. Usually extends from the surface to 2,500 feet AGL. LAANC applies during tower operating hours; outside those hours, the airspace typically reverts to Class E or G.

**Class E** — the default controlled airspace where no other class applies. No hard boundary color, but magenta vignette fading indicates where Class E begins at 700 feet AGL (common around smaller airports for instrument approaches). Class E extending to the surface is shown as dashed magenta. You need LAANC or a waiver to operate in surface Class E.

**Class G** — uncontrolled airspace, not explicitly colored on the chart because it's the absence of everything else. If there are no class boundaries overhead, you're likely in Class G. Drones can generally operate here without airspace authorization, up to 400 feet AGL, subject to all other Part 107 rules.

## Airports: Magenta vs. Blue

On a sectional, airports appear in two colors:

- **Magenta airports** — airports without an operating control tower, or airports that have a tower that doesn't operate 24/7.
- **Blue airports** — airports with a full-time operating control tower.

This matters because blue airports have active Class D airspace during tower hours. Magenta airports in Class E or G airspace may require less or no authorization depending on location.

## Obstacles and Terrain

Sectionals show terrain elevation through contour lines and gradient shading — darker green for low terrain, progressing through yellow and brown as elevation rises. This tells you the actual ground elevation you're operating above, which affects AGL calculations.

**Obstacle symbols** mark towers, antennas, and other tall structures with their height in two numbers: the top number is MSL height, the bottom (in parentheses) is AGL height. A tower marked `(449)` means it rises 449 feet above the ground at that location. Drone pilots should cross-reference these with local airspace apps — an 449-foot tower can easily exceed your 400-foot AGL ceiling.

## Special Use Airspace

Several overlay types represent restricted or conditional airspace:

- **Restricted Areas (R-)** — shown in blue hatching with an identifier like R-2508. Military operations may create hazards. Drone operations require coordination or are outright prohibited.
- **Military Operations Areas (MOAs)** — magenta hatching. Active MOAs involve military training; drone operations during active periods require ATC clearance.
- **Prohibited Areas (P-)** — solid blue boundary. Flight of any kind is prohibited (e.g., P-56 over the National Mall in Washington, D.C.).
- **Warning Areas (W-)** — similar to MOAs but over international waters.

Always check NOTAMs alongside sectional data — temporary restrictions like TFRs don't appear on the chart at all.

## How to Access Sectional Charts

The FAA's sectional charts are freely available through **SkyVector** (skyvector.com) as a browser-based overlay, or through aviation apps like ForeFlight. The FAA also publishes downloadable PDF versions through its website, updated on the 56-day cycle.

For drone-specific airspace planning, the sectional is the underlying data layer — but apps like **PreFlight 107** translate it into drone-relevant information: your current airspace class, the LAANC grid ceiling at your precise location, and active TFRs, all without needing to decode the full chart symbology from scratch.

## The Bottom Line

Sectional charts are the authoritative source for understanding U.S. airspace. Drone pilots who can read one have a meaningful advantage: they understand *why* the rules say what they say, can spot potential conflicts before consulting an app, and have a fallback when digital tools aren't available.

Learn the five airspace classes, the color codes, and how to spot obstacles — and the sectional goes from intimidating to genuinely useful.

*Fly safe out there.*
