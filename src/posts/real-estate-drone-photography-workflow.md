## The Difference Between One Job and a Standing Client

Two pilots shoot the same house. One arrives, flies around it for twenty minutes grabbing whatever looks nice, and sends 40 unsorted images three days later. The other checks conditions the night before, arrives with a shot list, is done in 25 minutes, and delivers 20 polished images the next morning in a branded gallery.

Both technically did the job. Only one gets called for the next listing — and the next twelve after that.

Real estate work is a **volume business built on reliability**, not artistry. The agent doesn't need your best creative expression; they need consistent, flattering, on-time images for a listing that goes live Thursday. Here's the process that delivers that every time.

## Phase 1: Before You Book — Qualify the Job

Two minutes here saves a wasted trip.

**Check the airspace at the property address, not the town.** A house can sit in Class D or under a LAANC grid with a 100-foot ceiling even in a quiet suburb. If you need authorization, you want to know now, not in the driveway.

**Look at the surroundings on a map.** Power lines, dense tree cover, a school next door, a busy road you'd be flying over, water. These change how you plan the shoot.

**Ask the agent three questions:** When does the listing go live (your real deadline)? What's the selling point — the acreage, the view, the pool, the proximity to something? And will anyone be on site?

## Phase 2: The Day Before — Check Conditions

This is the step that separates professionals from people who drive an hour for nothing.

- **Wind and gusts** against your aircraft's limits. Gusts matter more than sustained wind for image stability.
- **Ceiling and visibility** — a low overcast makes flat, gray images, and below minimums you legally can't fly at all.
- **Precipitation** and any storms building.
- **Sun position and time of day** — this is a creative decision with a hard schedule attached (more below).
- **TFRs** — they appear with no notice.

If it's marginal, **tell the agent early.** "Tomorrow looks windy, I'd rather shoot Thursday for better images" is a message that builds trust. Silently showing up and delivering mediocre work in bad light does the opposite.

## Phase 3: Timing — Light Is Most of the Job

The single biggest quality difference in real estate aerials isn't your drone, it's **when you fly.**

- **Golden hour** (shortly after sunrise, or the hour before sunset) is the standard for exteriors — warm, low, directional light that gives the house dimension and makes the lawn glow.
- **Midday** is the worst case: harsh overhead sun, hard shadows, blown-out roofs, flat-looking landscaping. Sometimes unavoidable, but never the first choice.
- **Overcast** is soft and even — perfectly workable, but flat. Fine for straightforward listings, wrong for a luxury property.
- **Twilight/dusk shots** — the house lights on against a deep blue sky — are the premium upsell. They take the most planning and command the most money. If you offer them, have the agent turn on every light in the house before you arrive.

Also: **face the light correctly.** Shooting with the sun behind you lights the house; shooting into it silhouettes the house and flares your lens.

## Phase 4: On Site — Fly the Shot List

Have a fixed list so you never drive home and discover you missed the angle they wanted. A reliable standard set:

1. **The hero shot** — front of the house, roughly 40–80 feet up, slightly angled rather than straight-on so the building has depth. This is the listing's lead image.
2. **The four corners** — orbit and capture each corner of the property so the agent can choose.
3. **A higher context shot** — showing the lot, the neighborhood, and how the property sits.
4. **The selling point** — whatever the agent told you in Phase 1. The pool, the acreage, the water view, the barn.
5. **Approach/setting shot** — the street and surroundings, giving buyers a feel for the area.
6. **Straight-down (top-down)** — excellent for showing lot boundaries and layout, especially on acreage.
7. **Video, if included** — a slow reveal pulling back and up from the house, plus a smooth orbit. Slow and steady always beats fast and dramatic here.

**Practical shooting notes:** shoot slightly wider than your final crop so you have room to straighten and reframe. Keep the horizon level. Avoid neighboring houses as the subject — frame so the listing is unmistakably the star, and be considerate about neighbors' windows and yards.

## Phase 5: Delivery — Fast and Professional

**Speed is a competitive advantage.** Agents work on listing deadlines; the pilot who reliably delivers next-day gets the standing gig even if their images are marginally less artistic.

Edit for **natural but polished**: straighten the horizon, correct exposure and color, lift shadows, and keep the sky believable. Skin the temptation to over-saturate — MLS listings have rules about misleading imagery, and a radioactive-green lawn looks amateur.

Deliver **web-sized and full-resolution** versions, cleanly named, in an organized gallery or folder — not a pile of DSC_0413 files. Include a short note with what's included and a reminder of the usage terms.

## Phase 6: Turn It Into a Repeat Client

- **Ask for the next listing** while they're happy: *"Send me the address whenever the next one comes up."*
- **Ask for a referral** to another agent in their office — real estate is a referral-dense industry.
- **Log the job** so you know what it actually took: flight time, drive time, editing hours. That's how you learn whether your package price is right.

## The Bottom Line

Real estate rewards **process over artistry.** Qualify the job by checking airspace at the actual address, check conditions the day before and speak up early if it's marginal, **shoot in golden hour whenever you can**, run a fixed shot list so nothing's missed, and **deliver fast and organized**. Then ask for the next listing. Do that consistently and you stop chasing one-off jobs — the listings just start showing up in your inbox.
