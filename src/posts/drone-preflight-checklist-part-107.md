## Crashes Are Usually Boring

When a drone goes down and it isn't weather, the cause is almost never exotic. It's a hairline crack in a prop nobody inspected. It's a home point that recorded in the parking lot instead of the launch site. It's a return-to-home altitude set to 60 feet with 80-foot trees between the aircraft and the pilot.

Every one of those is a checklist item. Which is exactly why checklists exist in aviation — not because pilots are forgetful, but because **routine is the thing that fails first.** After your fiftieth flight, the preflight is the easiest thing in the world to shortcut, and that's precisely when it starts to matter.

## It's Also the Law

Under Part 107, you're **required** to conduct a preflight inspection before every flight — assessing the operating environment, checking that the aircraft is in a condition for safe operation, verifying the control link, and confirming you have enough power to complete the intended operation. So this isn't optional best practice. It's a regulatory obligation, and "I always just eyeball it" isn't an inspection.

The regulation tells you *what* to check. Here's the practical version that tells you *how*.

## Phase 1: Before You Leave the House

The cheapest problems to fix are the ones you catch before you drive an hour to a site.

- **Weather at the actual site** — wind and gusts, ceiling, visibility, precipitation, temperature. Not "it looks nice outside."
- **Airspace check** — what class are you launching into? Do you need LAANC authorization? Any active TFRs?
- **Batteries charged** — aircraft *and* controller *and* your phone. All three end flights.
- **Firmware** — update at home on solid wifi, never in the field. A half-finished firmware update on a job site is a wasted trip.
- **SD card** — in the drone, formatted, with space. The number of pilots who've flown a perfect mission with no card in the aircraft is not small.
- **Props and spares** — packed, undamaged.
- **Documents** — Part 107 certificate, drone registration, insurance info. If you're flying commercially, you may be asked.

## Phase 2: On Site, Before You Launch

This is the inspection that catches the things that actually kill aircraft.

**The aircraft itself:**
- **Propellers** — this is the big one. Look for **cracks, chips, nicks, and bends**, and check they're seated and locked. A damaged prop can hold together on the bench and come apart at full RPM. When in doubt, replace it — props are the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.
- **Arms and airframe** — no cracks, nothing loose, motors spin freely with no grinding.
- **Gimbal and camera** — clamp removed, lens clean, gimbal moves freely and isn't fouled.
- **Battery** — seated and clicked in, no swelling, correct temperature.
- **Antennas** — controller antennas positioned properly (pointed so the flat faces are toward the aircraft, not aimed at it like a gun).

**The environment:**
- **Launch surface** — flat, firm, clear of loose debris, dust, sand, or tall grass that can foul motors.
- **Obstacles overhead and around** — power lines, wires, trees, buildings. Look *up*, not just around.
- **People** — who's nearby, and will anyone end up under the aircraft?

**The system checks — the ones people skip:**
- **GPS lock** — wait for a **strong satellite count** before you launch. Launching on a weak fix is how flyaways start.
- **Home point recorded at the launch site** — confirm it set *here*, not where you turned the drone on.
- **Return-to-home altitude set ABOVE the tallest obstacle** between you and the aircraft. This single setting has saved more drones than any other.
- **Compass/IMU** — calibrate if the aircraft asks, if you've traveled a long distance, or if you get a warning. Don't calibrate near metal, rebar, or vehicles.
- **Control link** — solid connection, live video feed, no interference warnings.
- **Flight-time plan** — know your realistic endurance in today's conditions and your landing threshold *before* you take off.

## Phase 3: The Go/No-Go

Then the actual decision. The checklist tells you the aircraft is airworthy; the go/no-go tells you whether *today* is the day. Wind versus your drone's limit, airspace clear, no storms building, and — honestly — are *you* fit to fly? Rushed, tired, or distracted is a real risk factor, not a personality trait.

If anything on the list is a "hmm," the answer is to fix it or not fly. There is no shot worth a lost aircraft.

## Phase 4: After You Land

Two minutes here saves you on the next flight:

- **Feel the battery and motors** — anything unusually hot is a warning.
- **Inspect props again** for damage picked up in flight.
- **Log the flight** — time, location, aircraft, conditions, anything unusual. This is your maintenance history and, if anything ever goes wrong, your evidence.
- **Note anything odd** — a new sound, a sluggish response, a warning that flashed. Little symptoms become failures.
- **Batteries to storage charge** if you're not flying again soon.

## The Bottom Line

Preflight checks are legally required and practically boring — which is exactly why they get skipped and exactly why they matter. **Check the weather and airspace before you drive out. On site, inspect your props like they're trying to lie to you, confirm GPS lock and your home point, and set RTH above the tallest obstacle.** Then make an honest go/no-go call, and log what happened when you land. Do that every time and most of the ways a drone gets lost simply stop being available.
