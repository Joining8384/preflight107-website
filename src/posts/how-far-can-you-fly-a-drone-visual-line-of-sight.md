Nearly every new pilot asks this, and nearly every answer they get is wrong. The box says 6 miles of transmission range. Someone in a forum says 400 feet. Someone else says half a mile.

The real answer is stranger and more useful: **Part 107 never names a distance.** It gives you a condition to maintain instead, and that condition is your own eyesight.

## What the rule actually says

Under **§107.31**, the remote pilot and anyone manipulating the controls must be able to see the aircraft throughout the entire flight well enough to:

- know where it is and how high it is
- know its attitude and direction of flight
- see and avoid other aircraft
- tell that it isn't a hazard to people or property on the ground

And here's the part that bites people: that has to be done with **unaided vision**. Corrective lenses are fine — glasses and contacts don't count as "aiding." Binoculars, a spotting scope, zooming the camera feed, and FPV goggles all do count, so none of them can be what keeps you legal.

So the legal limit on distance is simply this: **the point where you can no longer honestly do those four things.** That point moves. It changes with your aircraft, the light, the background, the weather, and your own eyes.

## Why your transmitter range is irrelevant

A 6-mile radio link tells you how far the aircraft can receive commands. It says nothing about whether you may send it there.

The two numbers aren't close. A Mavic-sized drone usually drops out of useful visual range somewhere around **1,500 to 2,500 feet** — under half a mile — while its radio keeps working many times farther. Treating the radio spec as a permission slip is how pilots end up with an aircraft they can't see, can't orient, and can't get out of the way of a traffic helicopter.

Video range deserves the same skepticism. A clean camera feed at 4,000 feet is not line of sight. The camera is not your eyes, and the rule is specific about that.

## Roughly how far is "too far"?

There's no official table, because it depends on the airframe. But it's worth having a mental scale for a typical folding camera drone:

| Distance | What you can usually tell |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ~500 ft | Orientation is obvious; you can see which way the nose points |
| ~1,000 ft | Still clearly an aircraft; orientation gets harder, especially nose-on |
| ~1,500 ft | A small dark dot; most pilots lose orientation here |
| ~2,000 ft | Hard to hold against a bright sky; vanishes entirely against trees or buildings |
| Beyond ~2,500 ft | Realistically gone for most aircraft, most conditions |

Two things shift that scale hard. **Size:** a sub-250g drone disappears far sooner than a large quad with bright strobes. **Background:** the same drone that's trackable at 2,000 feet against open blue sky can be invisible at 800 feet against a tree line, a rooftop, or a gray overcast that matches its own color.

## Find your own number

Don't guess at it — measure it once, and you'll know it forever.

1. Pick an open field on a clear day and launch.
2. Fly straight out at a fixed altitude, slowly, keeping your eyes on the aircraft rather than the screen.
3. The moment you can't confidently say which direction the nose is pointing, stop and read the distance off your controller.
4. Write that number down. That's your realistic VLOS limit for that aircraft in good conditions.
5. Then fly back and do it again with the sun in front of you. The second number is often dramatically shorter — and it's the one that matters on an afternoon job.

Most pilots are surprised by how early orientation goes, and how much earlier than "I can still see a speck" it happens. Seeing a dot isn't the standard; knowing its attitude and direction is.

## What quietly shrinks your range

- **Haze and humidity.** Reported visibility of 10 miles doesn't mean a 10-mile drone. Haze kills the contrast that lets you pick a small object out of the sky.
- **Looking into the sun.** The single biggest reducer. Early morning and late afternoon, flying toward the sun can cut your usable distance by more than half.
- **Flat gray overcast.** A dark gray aircraft against a dark gray sky is the worst contrast case there is.
- **Terrain and clutter.** Below the horizon line, against trees, hills, or buildings, a drone blends in immediately.
- **Twilight.** Legal to fly with anti-collision lighting, but your visual range collapses to roughly how far those lights are distinguishable — not how far the aircraft is.
- **Attention.** Glance down at the screen for five seconds at 1,800 feet and you may not re-acquire the aircraft at all. That's a real, common way flights go wrong.

## What a visual observer can and can't do

**§107.33** lets you use a **visual observer** — and this is where FPV goggles become legal, because the VO maintains the visual contact while you fly by camera.

A VO has to:

- keep the aircraft in **unaided** visual line of sight themselves
- scan for other aircraft and hazards
- stay in **effective communication** with you at all times

What a VO does *not* do is extend your range indefinitely. The requirement doesn't become "somebody saw it recently." Someone must have it in sight continuously, and be able to tell you right now. A VO can be positioned elsewhere, and communication can be by radio or phone — but if the link drops or they lose the aircraft, the operation has to stop, not continue on faith.

A practical note that gets skipped: brief your VO before launch on what they're watching for and exactly what words mean "land now." A VO who doesn't know the plan is a passenger.

## Going beyond line of sight

Flying **BVLOS** — genuinely beyond visual line of sight — is not something you decide on site. It requires a **waiver under §107.205**, with a real operational safety case: how you'll detect and avoid traffic, what happens on a lost link, who's responsible for what.

The FAA has been building a dedicated framework for routine BVLOS operations (the proposed **Part 108**) precisely because waivers don't scale. Where that stands by the time you read this is worth checking directly with the FAA rather than taking anyone's word for it, including mine. What hasn't changed is the baseline: **without an approval in hand, VLOS is the rule, and it's the rule for the entire flight.**

## Distance does more than break the rule

Flying far horizontally has knock-on effects pilots often miss.

Airspace is a map, not a bubble around you. Fly out 3,000 feet and you can cross from uncontrolled Class G into controlled airspace where you needed [LAANC authorization](/blog/what-is-laanc-drone-pilots) before you got there — your takeoff point being clear doesn't clear your flight path. The same goes for [TFR](/blog/what-is-a-tfr-drone-pilots) boundaries and the edges of a [controlled airspace class](/blog/controlled-vs-uncontrolled-airspace-drone-pilots).

Distance also eats battery asymmetrically. The leg home can be into the wind that helped you out, and a drone 2,000 feet away with 20% left is a much worse situation than the number suggests. If you do lose the aircraft, [what to do about a flyaway](/blog/drone-flyaway-what-to-do) is a decision tree worth having read beforehand rather than improvising.

## The short version

- There is no legal distance number — the limit is what you can actually see unaided.
- Your transmitter range and your legal range have nothing to do with each other.
- For most camera drones, honest VLOS runs out somewhere under half a mile, and much sooner in bad light or against clutter.
- Measure your own limit once, in good light and into the sun, and fly to the smaller number.
- A visual observer makes FPV legal and extends coverage, but someone must have the aircraft in sight continuously.
- BVLOS needs a waiver, not an opinion.

PreFlight 107 won't tell you how far you can see, but it does cover the conditions that decide it. The **visibility and ceiling** in your preflight check come from the nearest station's live report, the **Fly Now Score** weighs low visibility into its go/no-go read, and the **map** shows the airspace and TFR boundaries sitting around your launch point — so you can see which direction gets expensive before you fly that way.

Your eyes are the regulation. Everything else is just equipment.
