## The short version

Under **14 CFR 107.9**, a Part 107 remote pilot must report an operation to the FAA within **10 calendar days** if it resulted in either:

1. **Serious injury to any person**, or any **loss of consciousness**; or
2. **Damage to property other than your drone** exceeding **$500** to repair, or $500 in fair market value if it's a total loss.

If neither happened, you don't file anything. That's the whole test — and it means the overwhelming majority of crashes require no report at all.

## What the $500 rule actually means

This is where pilots get confused, so read it carefully: **damage to your own aircraft does not count.**

You can put a $2,000 drone into a lake and owe the FAA nothing. The rule says "property *other than the* small unmanned aircraft." Your drone is the one thing excluded.

What counts is what you hit. A cracked windshield, a dented car panel, a damaged roof, a broken window, someone's fence. The threshold is **cost of repair including parts and labor** — or, if the item is destroyed outright, its **fair market value**.

And $500 is low. A single automotive windshield or a decent camera lens clears it easily. Don't eyeball this — if you damaged someone's property, get an actual repair estimate before deciding you're under the line.

## What counts as "serious injury"

The FAA ties this to a recognized medical standard (Level 3 or higher on the Abbreviated Injury Scale). In practice, the useful question is: **did the person require hospitalization?**

A broken bone, a laceration deep enough for serious treatment, anything that puts someone in a hospital bed — that's reportable. A scratch, a scrape, or a bruise from a prop is not.

**Any loss of consciousness is reportable on its own**, regardless of how minor it looks afterward. If someone was knocked out even briefly, you file.

## Ten calendar days — not business days

The clock starts at the accident, and it counts weekends and holidays. A Friday-evening crash gives you until the second Monday, not later. Don't burn a week deciding whether it qualifies; get the repair estimate quickly.

## How to actually file the report

Two accepted paths:

- **FAADroneZone** — the same portal you used to register. There's an accident reporting section.
- **Your local FAA Flight Standards District Office (FSDO)** — by phone.

Either way, have this ready:

- Your name and contact information
- Your **remote pilot certificate number**
- The drone's **FAA registration number**
- Date, time, and location of the accident
- Any people injured and the extent of the injuries
- Property damaged and the extent of the damage
- A description of what happened

That last item matters more than pilots expect. "Lost link and it descended into a parked car" reads very differently from a vague, half-remembered summary written nine days later.

## Recreational flyers

Section 107.9 governs **Part 107** operations. If you were flying recreationally under the exception for limited recreational operations, that specific rule isn't the one binding you.

That is *not* a free pass. Serious accidents can trigger separate NTSB reporting obligations regardless of which rule you flew under, and state and local law — property damage, personal injury liability — applies to everyone with a drone in the air. If someone was hurt or you damaged property, talk to your insurer, and don't assume the recreational label makes it disappear.

## The first ten minutes matter most

Whether or not the crash turns out to be reportable, what you do immediately determines how well the next few weeks go:

- **Check on people first.** Everything else is secondary.
- **Photograph everything before it moves** — the drone, the damage, the surroundings, the sky conditions. Wide shots and close-ups.
- **Get contact info** from the property owner and any witnesses, while they're still standing there.
- **Write down what happened now**, while it's fresh. Time, altitude, what you saw, what the controller showed, when you lost link.
- **Note the conditions** — wind, gusts, visibility at the moment it happened.
- **Don't speculate about fault** to bystanders. Describe facts, not conclusions.

The pilots who come out of this cleanly aren't the ones who never crash. They're the ones who can show exactly what the conditions were, that they'd checked them beforehand, and that the flight was planned rather than improvised.

## Where PreFlight 107 fits

An accident report asks for date, time, location, and what the conditions were. If that information lives only in your memory nine days later, you're reconstructing — and reconstruction reads as guesswork.

PreFlight 107's flight logs capture your **launch and landing with a server-stamped time and a METAR snapshot from the moment you flew** — so the weather in your report is the weather that was actually recorded, not what you remember. Pair that with a Mission Briefing generated before takeoff and you can show the FAA both halves: that you assessed conditions beforehand, and what they actually were when it went wrong.

Nobody plans a crash. But the pilot who can produce a timestamped record of a properly planned flight is in a completely different position from the one who can't.

## Bottom line

Report within 10 calendar days if someone was seriously injured or lost consciousness, or if you did more than $500 of damage to property other than your own drone. Otherwise, no report is required. Either way: check on people, document everything immediately, get a real repair estimate before you decide you're under the threshold — and keep flight records good enough that you're never reconstructing from memory.

*This is general information for Part 107 pilots, not legal advice. For a serious accident, talk to an aviation attorney and your insurer.*
