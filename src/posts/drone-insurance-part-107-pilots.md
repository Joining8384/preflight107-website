## The Question Every New Commercial Pilot Hits

You landed the gig. A property manager wants roof inspection photos, or a realtor wants aerials of a listing. Then comes the email: *"Can you send over your certificate of insurance before you come out?"*

And you realize you have no idea what that means.

Drone insurance is one of those topics that feels intimidating because it's wrapped in jargon — liability, hull, COI, additional insured — but underneath it's straightforward. Once you understand the two coverages that matter and the one document that gets you hired, the whole thing clicks. Let's demystify it.

## Do You Legally Have to Carry Drone Insurance?

Start with the honest answer: **the FAA does not require you to carry insurance to fly under Part 107.** Unlike manned commercial aviation, there's no federal mandate for small-drone operators to hold a policy.

But "not legally required" and "not necessary" are very different things. Here's who effectively requires it anyway:

- **Your clients.** Most commercial clients — property managers, construction firms, realtors, event venues — won't let you on-site without proof of insurance. It's a checkbox in their risk process, not yours.
- **Some states and municipalities**, which have their own rules for commercial operations on public or permitted land.
- **Common sense.** If your drone falls out of the sky onto a car, a roof, or (the nightmare) a person, you are personally liable for the damage. Without insurance, that comes out of your own pocket — and a single serious claim can be tens of thousands of dollars.

So while nobody from the FAA will ask for your policy, the market absolutely will. If you intend to fly for money, treat insurance as the cost of doing business.

## The Two Coverages That Actually Matter

Almost every drone policy comes down to two types of coverage. Understand these and you understand 90% of drone insurance.

### 1. Liability coverage (the one clients care about)

**Liability** covers damage *you* cause to other people or their property — the third party. If your drone injures a bystander or damages a building, liability is what pays. This is the coverage clients require, because it protects *them* from being dragged into a lawsuit over your operation.

This is the non-negotiable one. If you buy nothing else, buy liability.

### 2. Hull coverage (the one that protects *you*)

**Hull** covers *your own equipment* — the drone itself, and sometimes payloads like cameras and gimbals. If you fly your $3,000 aircraft into a tree or lose it to a flyaway, hull coverage helps you replace it.

Hull is optional and comes down to math: if you're flying a $400 drone, it's often cheaper to self-insure (just eat the loss if it crashes). If you're flying a $10,000 mapping rig, hull coverage starts making a lot of sense.

## Annual vs. On-Demand: Which Fits You

There are two ways to buy this coverage, and the right one depends on how much you fly.

**On-demand (pay-per-use):** You buy coverage by the hour, day, or job — turning it on only when you fly. Providers like this are popular with part-time and weekend pilots because you're not paying for coverage on the 340 days a year you're not flying. If you do a handful of paid jobs a month, on-demand is usually cheaper.

**Annual policy:** A traditional yearly policy that covers you continuously. This makes sense once you're flying regularly — the per-flight cost drops well below on-demand, and you can generate certificates of insurance instantly whenever a client asks (instead of scrambling to activate a policy the morning of the shoot).

A common path: start on-demand while you're building the business, switch to annual once you're booking work weekly.

## The Certificate of Insurance (COI): Your Golden Ticket

That email asking for "proof of insurance"? What they want is a **Certificate of Insurance (COI)** — a one-page document from your insurer that proves your policy is active and shows your coverage limits.

Two things clients often ask for on the COI:

- **A specific coverage limit** (usually $1 million), and
- To be named as an **"additional insured"** — meaning your policy also protects *them* for that specific job.

The ability to generate a COI on demand is a real competitive advantage. The pilot who can email a properly-filled COI within five minutes of being asked looks like a professional. The one who says "let me figure out how to get that" looks like a hobbyist — and loses the gig. This is exactly why regularly-working pilots lean toward annual policies: instant COIs.

## How Much Coverage Do You Actually Need?

The market standard for drone liability is **$1 million**, and that number covers the majority of commercial jobs. Some larger clients — construction sites, utilities, government work — will require **$2 million or more**, and they'll tell you the number in the contract.

Rule of thumb: carry **$1M as your baseline** and be ready to bump it up for a specific job that demands it. Many on-demand providers let you raise limits per-flight, so you don't need to over-buy year-round for one big client.

## What Drone Insurance Won't Cover

Insurance is not a substitute for flying legally. Policies typically **exclude claims arising from illegal operations** — so if you crash while flying in restricted airspace, above 400 feet, beyond visual line of sight without a waiver, or otherwise outside your Part 107 authority, your insurer can deny the claim. You'd be uninsured *and* facing an FAA enforcement action.

The takeaway: insurance protects you when you're operating legally and something goes wrong. It does **not** protect you from the consequences of operating illegally. Compliance comes first; insurance is the backstop.

## The Bottom Line

You don't legally need drone insurance to fly Part 107 — but you need it to get *paid*. Carry **liability** coverage ($1M is the standard), add **hull** if your gear is expensive, choose **on-demand** while you're part-time and **annual** once you're booking regularly, and keep a **COI** ready to send in minutes. Just remember what it can't do: insurance backs you up when you fly legally and something goes wrong — it won't rescue a flight that shouldn't have happened.
