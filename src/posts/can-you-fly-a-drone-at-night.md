## The Shot You Can Only Get After Dark

The best light of the day is the light most pilots never fly in. The city skyline glittering after sunset. A stadium under the lights. Car trails streaking down a highway in a long exposure. A property lit up warm and golden for a real-estate listing that needs to *sell* the evening ambiance. The most cinematic drone footage often lives in the half-hour after the sun goes down and the hour after full dark.

For a long time, getting that shot legally was a hassle. Under the original Part 107 rules, flying a drone at night required a **specific FAA waiver** — you'd file a request, explain your safety mitigations, and wait weeks (sometimes months) for the FAA to approve or deny it. Plenty of pilots either skipped the paperwork and flew illegally, or just never shot at night at all.

That's no longer the case. As of a 2021 rule change, **you can fly a drone at night under Part 107 with no waiver at all** — *if* you meet two specific conditions. This post lays out exactly what those conditions are, what the rules require of your aircraft and your training, how recreational flyers fit in, what the FAA actually means by "night" and "civil twilight," and the one practical challenge that gets harder in the dark regardless of what the regulations say.

## The Short Answer: Yes, Since 2021

In a rule that took effect in **April 2021**, the FAA amended Part 107 to **allow routine night operations without a waiver**. Before that, night flight fell under a daylight-operations restriction that you had to waive. After the rule change, the waiver requirement for night flying went away — replaced by two standing conditions baked into the regulation itself.

So the headline is simple: **a Part 107 remote pilot can legally fly at night, no waiver needed, as long as both of the following are true.**

## The Two Conditions

### 1. You've completed the updated training

The first condition is on **you, the pilot**. To fly at night under Part 107, your aeronautical knowledge has to cover night operations. In practice that means:

- If you've passed the **initial Part 107 knowledge test** recently, the current version already includes night-operation content.
- If you're an existing certificate holder, you satisfy this by completing the FAA's **updated recurrent training** — the free online course every Part 107 pilot takes to stay current. The current recurrent training includes a module on night operations.

The point is that night flying carries real, distinct risks — judging distance and orientation in the dark, spotting other aircraft, recognizing illusions — and the FAA wants every pilot operating at night to have been trained on them. Make sure your recurrent training is current and covers night ops before you launch after dark.

### 2. Your drone has anti-collision lighting

The second condition is on the **aircraft**. To fly at night, your drone must be equipped with **anti-collision lighting that is visible for at least 3 statute miles**, with a **flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision**.

This is the requirement pilots most often get wrong, so be precise about it:

- **The little navigation LEDs built into your drone are almost never enough.** The green/red/white indicator lights on a typical consumer drone are designed to help you see orientation up close — they are not rated to be visible from three miles away. Don't assume your drone is night-legal out of the box.
- **You almost certainly need a dedicated strobe.** The standard fix is a small, bright **anti-collision strobe light** (they clip or stick onto the drone, weigh a few grams, and cost very little). A proper strobe is designed to hit that 3-statute-mile visibility and flash at a collision-avoidance rate.
- **It has to actually be on and flashing.** Steady-on lights don't satisfy the "flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision" language — the light needs to pulse so other aircraft can detect it against background lights.

The purpose is mutual visibility: at night, a manned aircraft can't see your drone the way it might catch a glint of it in daylight, so the strobe is what announces your drone's position in the sky.

## What About Recreational Flyers?

If you fly **recreationally** (under the Exception for Limited Recreational Operations, not Part 107), night flying is also allowed — but the lighting requirement comes through a slightly different door. Recreational flyers must follow the safety guidelines of an FAA-recognized **community-based organization (CBO)**, and the FAA expects those guidelines to require **anti-collision lighting for night operations — commonly visible for 3 statute miles**, the same standard Part 107 uses. So it's not a flat federal "3 miles" line the way Part 107 spells it out; it flows from the CBO guidelines you're required to follow. The practical answer is identical, though: put a strobe on it.

The training piece also works differently: to fly recreationally at all you must pass **TRUST** (The Recreational UAS Safety Test) and carry proof of it. But the bottom line for night is the same as Part 107 — **a flashing anti-collision strobe before you fly after dark.** The "I'm just a hobbyist" mindset doesn't exempt you from lighting up.

## What "Night" and "Civil Twilight" Actually Mean

The regulations don't just say "when it's dark" — they use defined terms, and the in-between period matters.

- **Night** is the period between the **end of evening civil twilight** and the **beginning of morning civil twilight**, as published in the Air Almanac. In plain terms: the genuinely dark hours.
- **Civil twilight**, for Part 107 purposes, is a fixed window written into the rule (§107.29(c)): the **30 minutes after sunset** and the **30 minutes before sunrise**. In the lower 48 it's exactly 30 minutes — Alaska uses the times published in the Air Almanac instead. It's that "not quite day, not quite night" light.

Here's what trips people up: the anti-collision lighting requirement applies to **both** true night **and** civil twilight. So if you launch 20 minutes after sunset thinking "it's still kind of light out, I'm fine," you're in civil twilight — and your drone **still needs the 3-mile anti-collision strobe**. The lighting requirement kicks in as soon as the sun is down, not only once it's pitch black.

## The Rules That Do NOT Change After Dark

Night flying relaxes one restriction (the old waiver) and adds one (lighting). Everything else about legal drone operation stays exactly the same — and forgetting that is where pilots get into trouble:

- **You still can't exceed 400 feet AGL.** Darkness doesn't raise your ceiling.
- **You still need airspace authorization in controlled airspace.** A Class B/C/D or surface-Class-E launch point requires LAANC authorization at 9 p.m. just like it does at noon. (And many LAANC grids are available 24/7, but you still have to get the authorization.)
- **TFRs still apply.** A stadium TFR for a night game, a VIP movement, a wildfire — all in force after dark.
- **You still must keep the drone within visual line of sight.** More on this below — it's the hard part.
- **The rules on flying over people and moving vehicles still apply** under the same operational categories.
- **You still can't fly recklessly or over people who aren't part of the operation** without meeting the relevant requirements.

Night isn't a loophole that suspends the rest of Part 107. It's daylight operations *plus a strobe and night training* — with everything else intact.

## The One Thing No Rule Change Can Fix: Seeing Your Drone

Here's the practical reality that matters more than any regulation: **maintaining visual line of sight is genuinely harder in the dark.**

The rule still requires that you (or a visual observer) keep the aircraft within unaided visual line of sight at all times. In daylight you can track the drone's body, its orientation, and the airspace around it. At night, the drone itself nearly disappears — what you're really tracking is your own anti-collision strobe, and it's surprisingly easy to lose orientation, misjudge distance, or confuse your drone's light with a distant light on the ground or another aircraft.

This is why the anti-collision strobe does double duty: it's not just so others can see you, it's so **you** can keep track of your own aircraft. Practical night-flying habits that keep you safe and legal:

- Fly sites you already know in daylight, so you understand the obstacles (towers, wires, trees) you can't see at night.
- Keep the drone closer than you would in daylight — don't push range when you can barely see it.
- Use a bright strobe with a distinctive flash so you don't lose it among streetlights and building lights.
- Have a visual observer if the operation is at all complex.

The FAA can remove a waiver requirement, but it can't make the dark easier to see in. That part is on your airmanship.

## Where PreFlight 107 Fits Your Night Flight

A night flight has more moving parts than a daytime one — the sun is setting on a clock, the lighting requirement just switched on, and the airspace and weather checks still have to happen. PreFlight 107 is built to make the night-specific pieces routine:

- **Night Ops Mode** — a sunset countdown so you know exactly when civil twilight and night begin (and therefore when your anti-collision lighting becomes mandatory), plus an anti-collision-lighting (ACL) verification checklist so you don't launch having forgotten the strobe. It turns "wait, is it legally night yet?" into a glance.
- **Live airspace + LAANC** — because controlled-airspace authorization is just as required at night, and you want to confirm your launch point and ceiling before dark.
- **Drone-specific weather** — wind, gusts, and visibility don't stop mattering after sunset; a clear, calm night is the goal, and conditions can shift as the temperature drops.
- **Real-time TFRs** — a night-game stadium TFR or a fresh restriction is exactly the kind of thing you want flagged before you set up in the dark.
- **Flight logging + evidence packets** — a clean record of a compliant night operation, including conditions at launch, in case anyone ever asks.

The goal is simple: handle the night-specific legal checks fast, so you can spend your attention on the genuinely hard part — keeping eyes on your aircraft in the dark.

## The Bottom Line

Yes — you can fly a drone at night, and you no longer need a waiver to do it. Under Part 107, two conditions make it legal: **completed night training** (current recurrent training or a recent knowledge test) and **anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles, flashing**. Recreational flyers can fly at night too, with the same 3-mile lighting requirement. Remember that the lighting rule kicks in the moment the sun sets — through civil twilight, not just full dark — and that every other Part 107 rule (400 feet, airspace authorization, TFRs, and especially visual line of sight) applies exactly as it does in daylight.

Get the strobe, keep your training current, check the airspace and the sunset clock, and that after-dark shot is yours — legally.

Plan it. Fly it. Prove it.

*Fly safe out there.*
