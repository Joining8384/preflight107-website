## The Number Nobody Will Tell You

Every new Part 107 pilot hits this wall. You've got the certificate, you've got the drone, someone finally asks "what do you charge?" — and you panic and say a number that sounds reasonable out loud. Usually it's too low. Then you deliver four hours of work for it and quietly resent the job.

The problem is that "what should I charge" gets answered with numbers, when it should be answered with a **method**. Numbers vary wildly by market, service type, and who you are. The method doesn't. Let's build it.

## Start With What It Actually Costs You

Before you can price anything, you need to know your real cost to operate. Most pilots dramatically underestimate this because they only count the flight.

**Your equipment has a lifespan.** A drone, controller, batteries, filters, cases, and a computer capable of editing 4K aren't a one-time purchase — they're a recurring cost spread over a couple of years. Batteries especially: they're consumables with a few hundred cycles in them, and they die on a schedule.

**Then the business costs:** insurance (annual or per-flight), software subscriptions for editing and mapping, storage for footage, your website, vehicle and fuel, and the taxes you owe on every dollar (as a self-employed operator, set aside meaningfully more than you think).

**And the time nobody bills for.** This is the big one. A "one-hour shoot" is realistically: 30 minutes of pre-flight planning and airspace checks, 30–60 minutes of driving each way, an hour on site, and then **two to four hours of editing and delivery**. If you charged for one hour, you just worked six hours for one hour's pay.

Add it up, divide by the hours you can realistically bill in a month, and you get your true break-even. Almost everyone is shocked by this number the first time.

## Why You Should Price Per Project, Not Per Hour

Hourly pricing is the trap. Two reasons:

**It punishes you for being good.** The pilot who nails the shot in 20 minutes because they're experienced earns less than the one who fumbles for two hours. Your speed is a skill you paid for; hourly billing turns it into a discount.

**It focuses the client on the wrong thing.** "$150/hour" invites the question "how many hours?" — a negotiation about your time. "$400 for the property package, delivered in 48 hours" invites a decision about the outcome. Clients aren't buying your hours; they're buying photos that sell a house.

**Package your services instead.** Define a small number of clear deliverables — "20 edited stills, delivered in 48 hours," "stills plus a 60-second walkthrough video," "full package with twilight shots." Fixed scope, fixed price, fixed timeline. Everyone knows what they're getting, and your margin improves as you get faster.

## Anchor to the Value, Not the Flight

Here's the reframe that changes your pricing forever: **your client isn't buying drone footage. They're buying an outcome.**

A real estate agent isn't buying aerials — they're buying a faster sale and a listing that outshines the others, on a house where their commission is measured in thousands. A roofing company isn't buying photos — they're buying an inspection that didn't require putting a person on a ladder, with the liability that carries. A construction firm is buying documented progress they can show a client or an insurer.

When your price is anchored to *their* outcome, a few hundred dollars is trivially justified. When it's anchored to "an hour of flying," it feels expensive. Same work, completely different conversation.

## What the Market Actually Looks Like

Real numbers vary enormously by region, competition, and your portfolio — a major metro supports very different rates than a rural county. But the general shape holds:

- **Real estate photo packages** are typically the entry point and the most competitive — priced per property, scaling with home size, number of images, and whether video is included.
- **Video and cinematic work** commands more than stills, because editing is where the hours go.
- **Inspections** (roof, tower, solar, infrastructure) pay better than real estate, because you're replacing something dangerous or expensive and the client is buying risk reduction.
- **Mapping, surveying, and orthomosaics** are the top tier — they require specialized software, processing time, and expertise, and clients budget for them accordingly.

The pattern: **the further you get from "pretty pictures" and the closer to "solving an expensive problem," the better it pays.** If you're competing purely on aerial photos, you're in the most crowded, price-sensitive corner of the market.

## The Race to the Bottom (and How to Skip It)

There is always someone with a Mini and no insurance who'll do it for $75. You cannot beat that person on price, and you shouldn't try. What you can do is not be selling the same thing:

- **Be legitimately professional.** Part 107 certified, insured, able to produce a certificate of insurance within minutes. A serious client with a serious property doesn't want the uninsured guy anywhere near their liability.
- **Be reliable.** Show up, deliver on time, communicate. An astonishing amount of business is won by simply being the person who answers the phone and hits the deadline.
- **Look like a business.** Branded deliverables, a real invoice, documented flights. A client-ready report with your logo signals a different tier than a Dropbox link of raw files.
- **Specialize.** "Drone photography" competes with everyone. "Roof inspections for insurance adjusters" competes with almost no one, and those clients care about credentials far more than price.

## Don't Forget to Bill for the Real Costs

A few things new pilots routinely eat that they should be charging for:

- **Travel** beyond a set radius
- **Rush delivery** (a premium, not a favor)
- **Extra revisions** beyond what the package includes
- **Weather reschedules** — build a policy into your terms so a scrubbed day isn't purely your loss
- **Licensing** — if a client wants broad commercial usage rights to your footage, that's worth more than a single-use listing

## The Bottom Line

Stop guessing at a number. **Calculate your true cost** — gear depreciation, insurance, software, travel, and above all the unbilled hours of planning and editing. **Price per project, not per hour**, so you're selling an outcome instead of your time. **Anchor to the client's value**, not your flight time. And **don't compete with the uninsured $75 guy** — be insured, reliable, specialized, and professional enough that the comparison never comes up. Charge what the work is worth, and the work starts feeling worth it.
