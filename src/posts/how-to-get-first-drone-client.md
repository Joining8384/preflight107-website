## The Gap Between Certified and Working

Getting your Part 107 feels like the finish line. Then you land, look at your certificate, and realize nobody knows you exist. The certificate proves you *can* fly commercially — it doesn't hand you anyone to fly for.

And you hit the loop immediately: **clients want to see your work, but you don't have work because nobody's hired you.** Almost every working drone pilot got stuck here for a while. Here's how they got unstuck.

## Step 1: Build a Portfolio Before Anyone Pays You

You cannot sell an invisible service. Before you approach anyone, you need to be able to show *something* — and you don't need a client to make it.

**Shoot spec work.** Pick subjects you can legally and safely fly, and produce work as if you'd been hired: a local park, a downtown skyline, a friend's property, a small business's building (with their permission), a farm, a construction site from public property. Edit it to the standard you'd deliver to a paying client, not "good enough for Instagram."

**Do a few free or steeply discounted jobs — deliberately.** Not forever, and not for anyone who'll expect it permanently. Pick two or three targets that give you *usable portfolio pieces* and a testimonial: a friend's business, a local nonprofit, a small realtor doing their first listing. Be explicit about it: *"I'm building my portfolio, so this one's on me — all I ask is permission to use the footage and a short testimonial if you're happy."* That's a fair trade, and it converts to referrals more often than you'd think.

**Make it easy to see.** A simple one-page site or a clean Instagram/portfolio page with 10–15 strong images and one short video is enough. Nobody needs your life story — they need to see that your work looks professional and that you're reachable.

## Step 2: Pick the Right First Industry

Not all drone work is equally easy to land cold. For a first client, you want industries with **high volume, low barrier, and an obvious use for aerials.**

**Real estate** is the classic starting point for a reason: there are agents in every town, they always need marketing content, listings are constant, and the decision-maker is one person you can talk to directly. It's also the most competitive and most price-sensitive — so it's a great place to *start* and a bad place to *stay*.

**Local small businesses** are underrated. Restaurants, marinas, venues, wineries, storage facilities, car dealerships, golf courses — anyone with a physical location and a website that looks dated. They rarely think about drone footage until someone shows them what it looks like.

**Construction and roofing** pay better and value you more, because you're solving an actual operational problem — progress documentation, or an inspection nobody has to climb for. Slightly harder to break into, much better long-term.

**Events** (weddings, festivals, sports) can pay well but carry real regulatory weight — flying over people has strict category rules, so make sure you understand those before you accept crowd work.

My honest advice: **start with real estate or local businesses to build the portfolio, then move toward inspections and commercial work** where the rates and the relationships are better.

## Step 3: Outreach That Actually Works

Here's what beats every ad you could buy as a beginner: **show them the thing before you ask for the money.**

**The tactic that works:** find a business or listing you'd like to shoot, go shoot it legally (from public property, respecting airspace and privacy), edit two or three genuinely good images, and send them with a short note:

> *"Hi — I'm a Part 107 certified drone pilot here in [town]. I flew past your property and took a couple of shots I thought you might like. They're yours to use, no charge. If you ever want a full set for your listings or website, I'd love to help. — [name, phone]"*

That works because it inverts the normal sales dynamic. You're not asking for anything; you've already given them something useful. It's concrete, local, and impossible to ignore the way a cold email is.

**Other things that genuinely work:**
- **Show up in person.** Real estate offices, local businesses. A card and a tablet with your best work beats an email nobody opens.
- **Ask everyone you know.** Your first client is very often someone's cousin who flips houses. Tell people what you do — specifically, not vaguely.
- **Join local business groups**, Chamber of Commerce, BNI-style referral groups. Being the one drone person in a room of local businesses is a great position.
- **Be findable.** A Google Business profile and a simple site so "drone photographer [your town]" turns you up.

**What mostly doesn't work at the start:** mass cold emails, broad social ads with no portfolio, and waiting for a marketplace app to send you work.

## Step 4: Look Like a Business From Day One

The gap between "guy with a drone" and "hire-able professional" is mostly presentation, and it's cheap to close:

- **Be insured**, and be able to send a certificate of insurance in minutes. Serious clients ask, and the ones who don't ask are often the ones who won't pay.
- **Have a simple contract** so scope and payment are in writing.
- **Deliver professionally** — a branded, organized delivery, not a Dropbox link of unnamed raw files.
- **Communicate like a pro.** Answer fast, confirm the appointment, tell them when to expect delivery, then hit that date. A shocking amount of work is won by simply being reliable.

## Step 5: Turn One Client Into Five

Your first client is worth far more than their invoice — if you treat them right:

- **Ask for a testimonial** while they're happy, immediately after delivery.
- **Ask for referrals explicitly:** *"If you know another agent who'd want this, I'd really appreciate the introduction."* People rarely refer unprompted, and almost always say yes when asked.
- **Get the repeat.** One listing becomes every listing if you're easy to work with. Recurring clients are the whole business — chasing new ones forever is exhausting.

## Where PreFlight 107 Fits

The thing that turns a first job into a second one is looking like a professional operation, and that's mostly documentation. PreFlight 107 gives you **Mission Briefings** and the **Client Bundle** — a branded, tamper-verifiable report showing exactly what you planned, the conditions you flew in, and proof it was done properly. Handing that to a client alongside the photos is the moment they stop seeing "a guy with a drone."

Your **Pilot Card** and public profile give clients a way to verify your credentials on the spot, and your flight logs quietly build the track record you'll point to when a bigger client asks how much experience you have.

## The Bottom Line

Break the no-portfolio loop by **making work nobody hired you for** — spec shoots and two or three deliberate free jobs in exchange for footage rights and a testimonial. **Start with real estate or local businesses**, then graduate toward inspections where the money and relationships are better. For outreach, **shoot first and give the images away** — it beats any cold email. Look like a real business from day one (insured, contracted, professional delivery), and then **squeeze every first client for a testimonial, a referral, and a repeat.** The first one is the hardest; the fifth one usually comes to you.
