## The Field That Was Legal Yesterday

You've flown this spot a dozen times. It's a public park two miles from your house — Class G airspace, nothing on the sectional, no airport nearby, no LAANC grid to worry about. You've made it your go-to for practicing orbits and shooting cloud timelapses. Last weekend you were out here and it was perfect.

Today you drive out, set up in the same corner of the same field, and launch. Nothing on your screen says anything is wrong. The drone climbs, you start your orbit, and everything is exactly like every other time.

What you don't know is that a wildfire ignited in the hills nine miles away two hours ago, and the FAA has thrown a **Temporary Flight Restriction** over a wide radius to protect the firefighting aircraft now working it. Your park is inside the ring. There are helicopters and air tankers operating low and fast in that airspace, and your drone is an invisible, unannounced hazard to a pilot who has enough to worry about. You are, at this moment, in violation of federal regulation — and you have no idea, because nothing in the field changed. The grass looks the same. The sky looks the same.

That's the defining danger of a TFR. It's the restriction that's **invisible until you've already broken it.** There's no fence, no sign, no controller on the radio telling a drone pilot to turn around. The airspace simply becomes illegal, silently, and the only way to know is to have checked before you left.

This post is about what TFRs are, the kinds that actually catch drone pilots, why their *temporary* nature is the whole problem, what happens if you bust one, and the habit that keeps you out of them.

## What a TFR Actually Is

A **Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR)** is a short-term rule that restricts aircraft operations in a defined volume of airspace, for a defined period of time, for a specific reason. They're issued by the FAA under federal regulation — **14 CFR §§ 91.137 through 91.145** — and they have the full force of law.

Crucially for you: **drones are aircraft, and TFRs apply to drones.** There is no carve-out for small unmanned aircraft. A TFR that prohibits flight in an area prohibits *your* flight in that area, Part 107 or recreational, LAANC authorization or not. A TFR overrides everything else — it supersedes a LAANC approval, it supersedes the fact that you're in Class G, it supersedes the fact that you've flown there a hundred times.

A TFR is defined by three things:

- **A geographic area** — usually a circle, described by a center point and a radius in nautical miles.
- **An altitude band** — typically from the surface up to some ceiling in feet AGL or MSL.
- **A time window** — an effective start and end, sometimes fixed, sometimes open-ended ("until further notice").

Each TFR is published as a **NOTAM** (Notice to Air Missions) and listed on the FAA's official TFR list at tfr.faa.gov. That's the legal mechanism: the FAA issues the NOTAM, and from that moment the airspace is restricted, whether or not any individual pilot has read it. Ignorance is not a defense — the duty to check is on you.

## The Common Types That Catch Drone Pilots

TFRs come in several flavors. A few of them are the ones drone pilots actually run into, and it's worth knowing each on sight.

### Stadium / Sporting Event TFRs

This is the single most common TFR a hobbyist or commercial drone pilot blunders into, because games happen in populated areas where people fly. Under a standing federal restriction, there is a TFR over **any stadium with a seating capacity of 30,000 or more** during:

- Major League Baseball games
- National Football League games
- NCAA Division I football games
- Major motor speedway events

The geometry is specific and worth memorizing: a **3 nautical mile radius** around the stadium, from the **surface up to 3,000 feet AGL**, effective from **one hour before** the scheduled event start until **one hour after** it ends. No drones, no exceptions, during that window. If you're shooting a real estate job or flying a park within three miles of a big stadium on game day, you can be perfectly legal at noon and illegal by the first pitch — same spot, same altitude, different time. This is the TFR that most embodies why "temporary" is dangerous.

### VIP / Presidential Movement TFRs

When the President, Vice President, or certain other protected officials travel, the Secret Service and FAA establish TFRs around their location and movement. These are often **large and tightly enforced** — frequently a strict inner ring (often around a 10 nautical mile radius) where essentially no general aviation or drone flight is permitted, surrounded by an outer ring with looser rules. VIP TFRs can appear with relatively short notice as travel is scheduled, can cover a major metro area, and are taken extremely seriously by enforcement. If a protected official is visiting your city, your usual flying spot may be inside the ring for a day or two with little warning.

### Disaster / Hazard / Firefighting TFRs

This is the wildfire scenario from the opening. When there's a wildfire, a major hazardous-materials incident, a flood, or another disaster requiring aerial response, the FAA issues a TFR to keep the airspace clear for the **firefighting aircraft, medevac helicopters, and law-enforcement aircraft** doing the response. These appear **fast** — a fire that wasn't there this morning can have a TFR over it by afternoon — and they exist precisely because manned aircraft are operating low and unpredictably in that space. A drone in a firefighting TFR is one of the most dangerous things you can do as a pilot: there are documented cases of wildfire air operations being **grounded** because a hobby drone was spotted, letting the fire grow while crews waited. These also carry the heaviest public and legal consequences.

### Security TFRs

Some areas have standing or special security TFRs — around certain government and military facilities, nuclear sites, and national landmarks (national-security and "no drone zone" restrictions). Special events of national significance (large summits, certain national celebrations) can also trigger temporary security TFRs. These are less common in day-to-day flying but blanket large, sensitive areas when active.

## Why "Temporary" Is the Whole Problem

It would almost be easier if TFRs were permanent. A permanent restriction is something you learn once and remember — like the prohibited area you know to avoid. You internalize it and you never fly there.

A TFR breaks that mental model in the most dangerous way: **the airspace's legal status changes, but nothing physical changes.** The defining hazards of a TFR all come from its temporary nature:

- **It's not on your sectional.** Sectional charts show permanent airspace structure. A TFR is, by definition, not permanent, so it does not appear on the chart you might use to plan. You can do a thorough airspace review of a printed or cached map and still miss a TFR entirely.
- **Yesterday's check doesn't count.** Because TFRs have time windows, the fact that the area was clear when you flew last week — or this morning — tells you nothing about right now. A firefighting TFR can stand up between your morning coffee and your afternoon flight. A stadium TFR switches on an hour before kickoff.
- **There's nothing in the field to warn you.** No tower will call you. No NOTAM gets read aloud over the grass. The boundary is an invisible cylinder in the sky. You can be standing legally and flying illegally in the same instant.
- **Familiarity is the trap.** The spots pilots bust TFRs in are almost always their *favorite* spots — the ones they've flown so many times they've stopped checking, because "I know this field." Familiarity is exactly what makes you skip the one check that would have caught it.

A TFR doesn't announce itself. It just quietly makes a place you trust into a place you can't be.

## What Happens If You Bust One

The consequences scale with the type of TFR and the harm, but none of them are minor.

- **FAA enforcement.** Violating a TFR is a violation of the federal aviation regulations. The FAA can pursue **civil penalties**, and the fines for TFR violations run high — published figures for serious TFR busts reach into the tens of thousands of dollars, with the most serious cases (security and VIP) carrying the steepest penalties. As a certificate holder, a Part 107 pilot also risks **certificate suspension or revocation**.
- **Criminal exposure.** Knowingly or recklessly violating certain security and VIP TFRs can carry **criminal penalties**, including the possibility of jail time, under the statutes that back those restrictions.
- **Interference with emergency response.** Fly a drone into a firefighting TFR and you can force the **shutdown of air operations** over an active fire. Beyond the regulatory penalty, that's a genuine danger to crews and communities — and it's the kind of incident that makes the news and the kind a court treats harshly.
- **The "I didn't know" defense doesn't work.** Because the TFR was published as a NOTAM and listed publicly, the FAA's position is that the information was available to you. Not having checked is not an excuse — it's arguably the violation. The duty to check before every flight is the standard you're held to.

The cost of checking is thirty seconds. The cost of not checking can be your certificate, a large fine, or a grounded air tanker. The math is not close.

## The Habit That Keeps You Out of TFRs

There's exactly one reliable defense, and it's a habit, not a tool: **check for active TFRs before every single flight, every time, even at your favorite spot — especially at your favorite spot.** Not because the airspace usually changes, but because the one time it has, nothing will warn you.

The official sources are the FAA TFR list at tfr.faa.gov and the NOTAM system. The problem is friction: cross-referencing a raw NOTAM list against your exact coordinates, in the field, every time, is the kind of chore that pilots quietly stop doing right around the time they've flown a spot enough to feel comfortable. The pilot doing it by habit and familiarity launches, flies a beautiful orbit, and never learns how close they came — or learns it the hard way when an FAA letter arrives weeks later, or worst of all when an air tanker has to break off because someone reported a drone.
