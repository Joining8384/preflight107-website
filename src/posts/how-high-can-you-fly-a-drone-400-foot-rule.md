## The rule, in one sentence

Under **14 CFR Part 107**, you may fly your drone up to **400 feet above ground level (AGL)**. Recreational flyers operate under the same 400-foot ceiling. That's the headline — but every important detail is in the words *"above ground level."*

## AGL, not sea level — this trips everyone up

The 400 feet is measured from **the ground directly beneath your drone**, not from sea level and not from where you're standing. This is **AGL (above ground level)**, and it matters the moment the terrain isn't flat.

Fly off the edge of a cliff or a steep hill and the ground drops away beneath you. Your drone might read "380 feet" on the controller — measured from your launch point — while it's actually hundreds of feet over the valley floor. Legally, altitude is measured against the ground *below the aircraft*, so a drone that's 380 feet above launch but 900 feet above the canyon bottom is a compliance problem, not a great shot.

The takeaway: on uneven terrain, your controller's altitude reading is relative to takeoff, and it can quietly disagree with the rule. Fly conservatively near drop-offs.

## The exception nobody remembers: the 400-foot structure rule

Here's the part most pilots miss. Part 107 lets you fly **higher than 400 feet AGL** in one specific case: when you're **inspecting a tall structure.**

If you're flying within **400 feet of a structure**, you may fly up to **400 feet above that structure's uppermost limit.** So a 1,200-foot broadcast tower can legally be inspected at 1,600 feet — as long as you stay within a 400-foot horizontal bubble of it.

The logic: tall structures are already a known obstacle that manned aircraft avoid, so a drone hugging one isn't introducing new risk. Step away from the structure, though, and you're back under the plain 400-foot AGL ceiling immediately.

## Why your ceiling is sometimes way *below* 400 feet

You've probably opened an airspace app near an airport and seen a grid capping you at 300, 200, 100 — or even **0** feet. That's not a bug. It's the **LAANC grid.**

In controlled airspace around airports, the FAA publishes a **UAS Facility Map** that sets the maximum altitude a drone can be auto-authorized to fly at each location. Close to a runway, that ceiling drops — sometimes to zero, meaning no automatic authorization at all. In those zones, **the LAANC ceiling replaces the 400-foot rule.** You don't get 400 feet just because Part 107 says so; you get whatever the grid says, and you need LAANC authorization to use it.

So the real ceiling over any given spot is the **lower** of:

- 400 feet AGL (the Part 107 baseline), and
- the LAANC grid ceiling, if you're in controlled airspace.

## Why 400 feet at all?

It comes down to staying out of the way of manned aircraft. Crewed airplanes and helicopters generally operate at **500 feet AGL and above** (with exceptions for takeoff, landing, and certain low operations). The 400-foot drone ceiling leaves a buffer between the two worlds. Bust it, and you're flying into the altitude band where real aircraft live — which is exactly the collision risk the whole system is built to prevent.

## Quick reference

- **Baseline:** 400 feet AGL, Part 107 and recreational alike.
- **Measured from:** the ground *below the drone*, not launch point or sea level.
- **Structure exception:** up to 400 feet above a structure's top, if within 400 feet of it.
- **Controlled airspace:** the LAANC grid ceiling can be lower than 400 — and it wins.
- **Never assume 400:** near airports, check the actual ceiling before you launch.

## Know your ceiling before you take off

The dangerous version of this rule is the pilot who assumes "400 feet, always" and pushes the sticks up near an airport where the real limit is 100. That's not a technicality — that's flying into approach paths.

PreFlight 107 shows you the **actual LAANC ceiling for your exact location**, whether you're in controlled airspace, and the current TFRs overhead — so the number in your head matches the number the FAA actually allows where you're standing. Four hundred feet is the rule; knowing when it isn't is the skill.

## Bottom line

You can fly a drone up to 400 feet above the ground beneath it — higher only when hugging a tall structure, and often *lower* when you're near an airport and the LAANC grid says so. Learn to read the ceiling for the spot you're actually in, and "how high can I fly?" stops being a guess.
