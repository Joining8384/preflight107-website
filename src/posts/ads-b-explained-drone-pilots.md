## What Is ADS-B?

ADS-B stands for **Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast**. It's a surveillance technology that allows aircraft to determine their own position via GPS and broadcast that information — along with speed, altitude, heading, and tail number — to anyone with a receiver nearby.

Unlike traditional radar, which requires expensive ground-based infrastructure to actively ping aircraft, ADS-B is passive and continuous. Equipped aircraft broadcast their position automatically, several times per second, without any prompting. Ground stations and other aircraft simply listen.

The FAA mandated ADS-B Out equipment on most manned aircraft operating in controlled airspace by January 1, 2020. That rule transformed ADS-B from a niche technology into the backbone of low-altitude traffic awareness in the United States.

## ADS-B In vs. ADS-B Out

Understanding ADS-B requires knowing the difference between its two sides:

- **ADS-B Out** — the transmitter. The aircraft broadcasts its position. All manned aircraft required to operate in most controlled airspace must have this.
- **ADS-B In** — the receiver. A device that listens for ADS-B Out signals and displays nearby traffic. This is optional for manned pilots and not required for drones.

As a drone pilot, you're on the *receiving* end. Your drone doesn't need to broadcast ADS-B — but having an app that receives and displays nearby ADS-B traffic gives you real-time situational awareness that would otherwise be impossible.

## Why It Matters for Drone Pilots

The airspace below 400 feet AGL is shared. Helicopters, agricultural aircraft, news and medical helicopters, banner-tow planes, and traffic aircraft all operate at low altitudes. Many of them are operating near airports, highways, and urban areas — exactly where drone pilots work.

Collisions between drones and manned aircraft are rare, but the consequences are severe. A one-pound drone striking a helicopter rotor or a small aircraft windshield at cruise speed is not a minor event. The FAA takes airspace conflicts seriously, and so should you.

ADS-B gives you one critical tool: *advance notice*. When you can see a helicopter inbound at 600 feet, two miles out, moving toward your position, you have time to descend, hold position, or abort the flight. Without that awareness, you're operating blind.

## What ADS-B Shows You

A good ADS-B display gives you the following for each tracked aircraft:

- **Position** — plotted on a map relative to your location
- **Altitude** — in feet MSL (mean sea level) or AGL depending on the app
- **Speed** — ground speed in knots
- **Heading** — direction of travel
- **Call sign or tail number** — identifies the aircraft or flight
- **Climb/descent rate** — whether the aircraft is ascending or descending

With this data, you can estimate the aircraft's trajectory and determine whether it poses a conflict with your operation — and act accordingly.

## What ADS-B Doesn't Cover

ADS-B is powerful, but treating it as a complete picture of the sky is a mistake. Several categories of aircraft may not appear on your display:

- **Pre-2020 aircraft without ADS-B Out** — some older, non-IFR aircraft still operate legally without it, particularly in Class G airspace
- **Helicopters at very low altitude** — some EMS and utility helicopters operate below ADS-B coverage areas or in terrain that degrades signal reception
- **Military aircraft** — many military operations are excluded from ADS-B tracking entirely
- **Gliders and ultralights** — not required to carry ADS-B Out in most airspace classes
- **Ground station gaps** — in remote or mountainous areas, ADS-B ground station coverage can have gaps, meaning aircraft may not appear until they're close

The absence of traffic on your display is *not* a guarantee that the sky is clear. ADS-B augments your visual scan — it doesn't replace it. Always maintain visual awareness of your surroundings.

## How to Use ADS-B in Practice

Effective use of ADS-B is about integrating it into your preflight and in-flight workflow, not just having the app open.

**Before takeoff:** Check your ADS-B display for traffic patterns in the area. If you're near a hospital, news station, or busy corridor, note which directions aircraft are typically approaching from. Look for any aircraft already established in patterns that could conflict with your planned operation.

**During flight:** Keep the display visible or have a visual observer monitor it while you fly. Set proximity alerts if your app supports them — a notification when a tracked aircraft comes within a defined radius of your position gives you reaction time.

**After an encounter:** If an aircraft passes closer than expected, log it. Note the time, the aircraft's call sign if visible, your position, and what action you took. This documentation matters if the encounter is ever reviewed.
