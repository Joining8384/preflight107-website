## What Is LAANC?

LAANC — pronounced "Lance" — stands for **Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability**. It's the FAA's automated system for granting near-real-time airspace authorization to drone pilots who want to fly in controlled airspace around airports.

Before LAANC launched in 2018, getting authorization to fly near an airport meant submitting a manual waiver application and waiting days — sometimes weeks — for a response. LAANC changed that. Now, eligible pilots can request authorization and receive a response in seconds, directly through an approved app.

## Why Controlled Airspace Matters

The airspace around most airports is controlled, meaning the FAA manages the flow of manned aircraft in and out. That airspace is divided into classes — Class B (major hubs like LAX or JFK), Class C (mid-sized airports), Class D (towers with local traffic), and portions of Class E (instrument approaches).

Flying a drone in any of these classes without authorization isn't just inadvisable — it's a federal violation. Under FAA Part 107, commercial drone pilots are prohibited from operating in controlled airspace without prior approval. Recreational pilots flying under the Exception for Recreational Flyers face the same restriction.

LAANC is how most pilots get that approval fast.

## How LAANC Works

LAANC is built on a grid system. The FAA defines **UAS Facility Maps** (UASFMs) — a patchwork of grid squares around airports, each with a pre-approved maximum altitude for drone operations. If your planned flight falls within a grid square and stays at or below its approved ceiling, LAANC can authorize you automatically.

Here's the typical flow:

- **Open a LAANC-enabled app** — PreFlight 107 or the FAA's DroneZone
- **Select your flight location** — the app checks it against the UASFM grid
- **Review the altitude ceiling** — each grid square shows the FAA-approved max (often 0 ft, 100 ft, 200 ft, or 400 ft)
- **Submit your request** — authorization comes back in seconds if you're within limits
- **Fly** — your authorization is logged and timestamped

If your location has a 0 ft ceiling, LAANC cannot automatically approve the flight. You'd need to apply through FAA DroneZone for a manual Part 107 waiver — a longer process reserved for special circumstances.

## Who Can Use LAANC?

LAANC is available to two groups:

- **Part 107 commercial pilots** — the primary users. Any flight in controlled airspace for commercial purposes requires authorization, and LAANC is the fastest path to getting it.
- **Recreational flyers** — those operating under the FAA's Exception for Recreational Flyers can also use LAANC, as long as they fly within the grid altitude limits and follow community-based safety guidelines.

LAANC does *not* cover every situation. It doesn't apply to flights above 400 feet AGL, or areas subject to Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs). Always check for active TFRs before flying, even if LAANC approves your altitude and location.

## When You Don't Need LAANC

Not every flight requires airspace authorization. If you're flying in **Class G (uncontrolled) airspace** — which typically covers rural areas away from airports — no authorization is needed, as long as you stay under 400 ft AGL and comply with all other Part 107 rules.

A quick rule of thumb: if there's no airport nearby and the sky above you isn't a controlled class, you're likely in Class G. But "likely" isn't certainty. Always verify with a current airspace map before takeoff.

## What LAANC Doesn't Cover

LAANC authorization is location-specific and real-time, but it's not a blanket permission slip. It doesn't override:

- **Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)** — presidential movement, wildfires, sporting events, and other TFRs supersede LAANC approval
- **Local ordinances** — some cities and municipalities ban drone flights regardless of FAA authorization
- **Night operations** — Part 107 night flights require either a waiver or completion of the updated aeronautical knowledge test; LAANC doesn't change that
- **Other Part 107 waivers** — flying beyond visual line of sight, over people, or from a moving vehicle still requires a separate waiver

Authorization to enter the airspace is one piece of the legal puzzle. The rest of Part 107 still applies in full.

## The Bottom Line

If you're flying anywhere near an airport, you almost certainly need LAANC authorization — and getting it takes less than a minute with the right tools. If you're in Class G airspace well clear of any airport, you don't.

The key is knowing which airspace you're in *before* you fly, not after you land.
