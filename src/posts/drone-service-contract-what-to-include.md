## The Clause You Learn the Hard Way

Ask a working drone pilot why they use contracts and you'll get a story, not a principle. The client who kept requesting "just one more small change" for three weeks. The shoot that got rained out twice, costing two days of driving with nothing to invoice. The realtor who used the footage for a listing — and then the brokerage used it in a regional ad campaign.

None of those people were villains. They just had a different assumption than the pilot did, and nothing written down to settle it. **That's what a contract is for: not distrust, but a shared answer to questions neither of you has thought about yet.**

For drone work specifically, a few clauses matter far more than the generic freelance template covers.

## The Core Clauses

**Scope of work — be specific.** "Aerial photography of the property" is an argument waiting to happen. "Up to 25 edited still images and one 60-second edited video of the exterior and grounds, single visit, approximately one hour on site" is a scope. Vague scope is the #1 source of unpaid extra work.

**Deliverables and format.** What exactly do they get, how many, at what resolution, in what file format, and delivered how? Spell it out.

**Timeline.** When you shoot, and when they receive the files. Give yourself an honest window — editing always takes longer than you think, and a missed self-imposed deadline damages a relationship you were trying to build.

**Payment terms.** Total price, and *when* it's due. Consider a **deposit up front** (commonly a portion of the total) for larger jobs — it filters out unserious clients and covers you if they vanish. State your invoice terms (due on delivery, net 15, net 30) and, if you like, a late fee. Ambiguity here is how you end up chasing money for months.

**Cancellation policy.** What happens if they cancel the day before? A common structure: cancel with reasonable notice, no charge; cancel same-day after you've already driven out, a partial fee. You're protecting your time, which is your inventory.

## The Two Clauses Drone Pilots Forget

These are the ones missing from generic contract templates, and they're the ones that specifically bite aerial work.

### 1. The weather / reschedule clause

**This is the one unique to your business.** You are entirely at the mercy of conditions — and you're also the one legally responsible for the go/no-go call. Your contract needs to say, plainly:

- **You** (the pilot) have sole authority to postpone for safety, weather, or airspace reasons, and that postponement is not a breach of contract.
- What happens next: how many reschedule attempts are included, and whether travel costs are recoverable if you've already driven out.
- That legal or safety limits — restricted airspace, an unexpected TFR, unsafe wind — mean the flight doesn't happen, period.

Without this, a client can reasonably feel you failed to deliver when you were actually doing your job correctly. With it, scrubbing a flight is a documented, expected part of the process.

### 2. Usage rights and licensing

Who owns the footage, and what can the client do with it? Under U.S. copyright, **you as the creator generally own the images you make** unless you sign that away. Most clients assume they're buying unlimited ownership; most pilots assume they're licensing a specific use. Both walk away happy and wrong.

Decide and write it down:
- **Limited license** — client may use the images for a specific purpose (this listing, their website, their social media). Cheaper. You keep the copyright and can use the work in your portfolio.
- **Broad/exclusive license or full buyout** — the client gets wide or exclusive rights, including ad campaigns and resale. This should cost meaningfully more, because you're giving up future value.

And explicitly reserve **your right to use the work in your own portfolio and marketing** — unless the client is paying for confidentiality. Losing portfolio rights by accident is a real and avoidable loss for a growing business.

## The Protective Clauses

**Insurance and liability.** State that you carry liability coverage (and the limit), and be clear about the boundaries of your responsibility. Many commercial clients will also want to be named as an **additional insured** — that request usually shows up here.

**Compliance and authority.** A short clause confirming you operate under Part 107 and applicable regulations, and that the operation is subject to airspace authorization. This does double duty: it signals professionalism, and it sets up the weather/airspace clause above.

**Site access and permission.** Make it the client's responsibility to secure property access and any permissions needed for you to be on site. You don't want to discover on arrival that the neighbor whose yard you need to launch from wasn't asked.

**Revisions.** State how many rounds of edits are included (one or two is typical) and what additional revisions cost. This single line prevents the most common form of unpaid overtime in creative work.

**Limitation of liability.** A cap on your total liability — commonly the value of the contract. Standard practice, and worth having.

## Keep It Human

A contract that reads like a lawsuit makes small clients nervous. For everyday jobs, a clear **one-to-two page agreement** in plain language does the job: what you'll do, what they get, when, for how much, what happens if the weather doesn't cooperate, and who can use the footage for what. Bigger commercial and enterprise clients will often bring their own paperwork — read it, especially the indemnification and usage-rights sections.

**Get it in writing, get it signed** (e-signature is fine and fast), and keep a copy with the job.

## The Bottom Line

Cover the basics — **scope, deliverables, timeline, payment, cancellation** — but don't skip the two clauses that are specific to flying: a **weather and reschedule clause** giving you sole authority to postpone for safety without breaching, and **usage rights** spelling out exactly what the client can do with the footage and preserving your portfolio rights. Add insurance, site access, and a revision limit, keep it to a readable page or two, and get it signed. It's not distrust — it's the document that lets you stay friendly when something unexpected happens.
