## Do you even need to register?

Before you pay anyone anything, confirm you're required to register at all. It comes down to weight and purpose:

- **Flying under Part 107 (commercial):** you must register, **regardless of weight** — even a 249g Mini.
- **Flying recreationally:** you must register only if your drone weighs **250g or more.** Under 250g flown purely for fun is exempt.

If you're not sure which lane you're in, that's the more important question to settle first — we broke it down in our post on whether sub-250g drones need registration. Once you know you need to register, the process below takes about ten minutes.

## Step 1: Go to FAADroneZone (and only FAADroneZone)

Register at the FAA's official portal, **FAADroneZone**. This matters: search results are full of third-party sites that charge $30–$80 to "help" you file a $5 government form. You never need them. Registration is $5, paid directly to the FAA, and valid for **three years.**

Create an account with your email, then verify it. Keep the login — you'll come back here to renew, add drones, or update your info.

## Step 2: Pick the right registration type — this is the fork

Inside DroneZone you'll choose between two registration types, and **they are not interchangeable:**

- **Part 107 (commercial).** Each drone is registered **individually**, with its own registration number. Fly ten drones commercially, register ten times.
- **Recreational (Exception for Limited Recreational Operations).** One $5 registration covers **all** the drones you fly recreationally — you register yourself once and mark every drone with the same number.

Here's the trap: a recreational registration does **not** make you legal for commercial flights, and vice versa. If you registered recreationally and later take a paid job, that drone needs a **Part 107 registration** — the recreational one doesn't count. Register for how you actually fly.

You'll also confirm you meet the age minimum: **13+** to register recreationally, **16+** for Part 107.

## Step 3: Enter your drone and Remote ID details

You'll provide the drone's **make and model**, and — importantly — its **Remote ID information.** Most current drones are **Standard Remote ID** aircraft: you enter the **Remote ID serial number** from the manufacturer (it's in the drone's app or on the box). If you're adding a separate broadcast module to an older drone, you'll enter that module's serial instead.

Getting this right at registration is what keeps your drone compliant with the Remote ID rule — the "digital license plate" requirement that applies to every drone that must be registered.

## Step 4: Pay the $5 and get your number

Pay the $5 fee and the FAA issues your **registration number** — it starts with **"FA"** followed by a string of characters. Save it. This is the number that proves your drone is legal, and you'll need it in the next step and any time a client or official asks.

## Step 5: Mark your drone (don't skip this)

Registration isn't finished when you pay. The FAA requires the **registration number to be displayed on the exterior of the aircraft**, readable without tools. A label, engraving, or permanent marker in the battery compartment's exterior or on the body all work — it just has to be legible and on the outside.

An unmarked but "registered" drone still gets you a violation, because an inspector can't verify it in the field. This is the step people most often forget.

## Step 6: Carry proof when you fly

Finally, you must have **proof of registration available** whenever you operate — a digital copy on your phone is fine. If law enforcement or an FAA inspector asks, you show the certificate. No proof on hand is its own problem, even if the drone is properly registered.

## Quick recap

- **Where:** FAADroneZone — the official $5 portal, never a third-party reseller.
- **Which type:** Part 107 = per drone; recreational = one registration covers all your drones. They don't cross over.
- **What you'll need:** make/model + Remote ID serial number.
- **After paying:** mark the number on the drone's exterior, and carry proof when you fly.
- **Renew** every three years.

## Where PreFlight 107 fits

Registration makes your drone legal to exist in the sky; it doesn't make any given flight legal. Once your FA number is on the aircraft, the per-flight questions still stand — are you in controlled airspace, does LAANC apply, is there a TFR overhead, are you under 400 feet?

That's the check PreFlight 107 runs before every launch, and your registration number lives right alongside your pilot details in the app — so when it's time to build a Mission Briefing or hand a client an FAA-ready document, the paperwork side is already done.

## Bottom line

Registering a drone is genuinely quick and cheap — $5, ten minutes, once every three years — as long as you pick the right path for how you fly, enter your Remote ID, and actually mark the aircraft. Do it on FAADroneZone directly, skip the resellers, and keep proof on your phone. Then the only thing left to check is the airspace above wherever you're standing.
