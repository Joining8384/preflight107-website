## The Trip Where Your Drone Stays in the Bag

Every traveling pilot has a version of this story: they hauled the drone across the country (or the world), and then never flew it — because the batteries got flagged at security, or the park banned it, or the country required a permit they'd never heard of.

None of that is hard to avoid. It just requires doing the homework *before* you pack, because every one of those problems is unfixable once you've arrived. Here's the whole picture.

## Flying With It: The Battery Rules Are the Real Rules

The drone itself is rarely the problem. **Lithium batteries are.**

**The one rule you cannot break: lithium batteries go in your CARRY-ON. Never in checked baggage.** This is a firm airline and aviation-safety requirement worldwide, and the reasoning is blunt — if a lithium battery goes into thermal runaway in the cabin, crew can deal with it; in the cargo hold, nobody can. Checked-bag lithium batteries are how you get a bag pulled, a fine, or worse.

Alongside that:

- **The drone can usually go in either**, but put it in your carry-on anyway. It's fragile and expensive, and baggage handlers are not gentle.
- **Protect the terminals.** Tape over contacts or keep each pack in its own **LiPo-safe bag** or sleeve so nothing can short against keys or coins.
- **Discharge to storage level** (~50%) before you travel. Safer, and it's better for the batteries anyway.
- **Watt-hours matter.** Most drone packs fall under **100 Wh**, which airlines accept without special handling. Between **100–160 Wh** you typically need **airline approval in advance**, and above **160 Wh** batteries are generally **not permitted** on passenger aircraft at all. Check the Wh rating printed on your pack — most consumer drones are comfortably under 100 Wh, but larger prosumer and cinema packs are not.
- **Airlines set their own quantity limits**, often around two spare batteries. Check your specific carrier before you show up with eight.

Give yourself extra time at security, and don't be surprised if they want to inspect the bag. A drone is an unusual X-ray shape; a calm "it's a camera drone, batteries are here" makes it a 60-second conversation.

## Domestic Travel: Same Rules, New Rules

Flying to another U.S. state doesn't change federal rules. Your **FAA registration, Remote ID, and Part 107 certificate travel with you**, and airspace works the same everywhere — check the class at your launch point, get LAANC authorization if you're in controlled airspace.

What *does* change is everything layered on top:

- **State and local laws vary a lot.** Some states have specific privacy statutes; many cities and counties restrict **takeoff and landing** from public parks and property. The FAA controls the airspace, but local governments can control the ground you launch from — which is a real constraint.
- **National Parks are a hard no.** Launching, landing, or operating a drone from within a U.S. National Park is prohibited, and rangers do enforce it. (The airspace above is FAA-controlled, but you can't take off or land inside the boundary — which for practical purposes means don't.)
- **Stadiums and sporting events** carry standing TFRs, and **wildfires** generate them without warning. Always check TFRs at your destination on the day.

The habit: treat a new city like a new airspace problem. Check the class, check TFRs, check whether the specific park or beach you're eyeing allows launching.

## International Travel: Do This Homework First

This is where people actually get burned, because **your U.S. credentials mean nothing abroad.** Your FAA registration doesn't transfer, and your Part 107 isn't recognized as a license in other countries.

Rules range from relaxed to severe:

- Some countries require **registration or a permit** before you arrive.
- Some require the drone be **declared at customs**.
- Some **prohibit drones entirely**, and will confiscate them at the border — occasionally with a fine attached.
- Many have strict rules around cities, heritage sites, airports, and government buildings, with real penalties.

Before any international trip: **look up the destination country's civil aviation authority** and confirm what's required. If you can't confirm it, assume the answer is restrictive. And be honest with yourself — for a short trip to a country with a permit process, the correct answer is often to leave the drone home.

## The Practical Packing List

- Drone in **carry-on**, in a hard or padded case
- All batteries in **carry-on**, at ~50% charge, terminals protected
- **Spare props** (they break, and the right size is hard to find abroad)
- **Chargers and cables** — plus an outlet adapter for international
- **Registration and certificate** — a PDF on your phone and, ideally, printed
- **Insurance documents** if you're working
- **ND filters / SD cards** — small, easy to forget, annoying to replace

## Where PreFlight 107 Fits

Travel is exactly when you're flying somewhere you don't know, which is when the airspace check matters most. PreFlight 107 gives you the **airspace class, LAANC ceiling, TFRs, and weather at wherever you're standing** — so an unfamiliar launch site is the same one-tap check as your home field. **Saved Sites** let you scout and store destinations ahead of the trip, and your **Hangar and documents travel with you**, so your registration, certificate, and insurance are in your pocket if anyone asks.

*(Airspace features are U.S.-focused — internationally, always confirm with the local aviation authority.)*

## The Bottom Line

Traveling with a drone comes down to three things. **Batteries in the carry-on, always** — under 100 Wh, terminals protected, at storage charge. **Domestically**, your federal credentials travel but local launch restrictions don't — and National Parks are off-limits. **Internationally**, your U.S. paperwork means nothing, so check the destination's aviation authority before you pack, because "I didn't know" doesn't get your drone back at customs. Do the homework once and the drone comes home with you, along with the shots.
