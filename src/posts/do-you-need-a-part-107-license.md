## The Clip That Did Numbers

You got the drone for your birthday. The first few weeks were pure fun — flying it over the backyard, scaring the dog, getting the hang of the sticks. Then one evening you drove out to the coast, launched into a golden-hour sky, and flew a slow push out over the water as the sun dropped. The footage was *stunning*. You cut it to thirty seconds, posted it that night, and woke up to a few thousand views and a comment section full of "where is this?!"

One of those comments was from a realtor in your town. She asks if you'd be willing to shoot a listing for her — a waterfront property going on the market next month. She'll pay. Three hundred bucks for an afternoon of flying the thing you'd happily fly for free.

And right there, in the gap between "yes, absolutely" and actually typing it, a quieter question surfaces. The one you've been not-quite-thinking about since the coastline clip started doing numbers.

*Wait. Am I even allowed to do this? Was the coastline video itself a problem? Do I need... a license?*

That hesitation is the single most common moment in a new drone owner's life. Almost everyone arrives at it eventually, usually right when something good is about to happen. And it's worth taking seriously, because the line you're standing on isn't a vibe or a guideline — it's federal aviation regulation, and which side of it you're on changes what's required of you, what you can do, and what happens if you get it wrong.

The good news: the rule is knowable. There are two paths, the test for which one applies to you is simpler than most people fear, and once you understand it you'll never have to wonder again. Let's walk it.

## Two Paths, One Question

Every drone flight in U.S. airspace falls under one of two regulatory frameworks. There is no third option, no "I'm just a guy" exemption, no gray middle where the rules don't apply. You are flying under one of these two:

**Path 1 — The Exception for Recreational Flyers.** This is the hobbyist path. It exists under federal law (49 U.S.C. § 44809) and it's specifically for people flying *purely for fun*. No business purpose, no payment, no professional benefit — just the joy of flying. If that's genuinely all you're doing, this path has light requirements and you can be fully legal in an afternoon.

**Path 2 — Part 107.** This is the commercial path, governed by 14 CFR Part 107 (the "small UAS rule"). It's required for *any* flight that isn't strictly recreational. It involves a real aeronautical knowledge test, a real certificate, and ongoing currency requirements. It's more work — but it also unlocks the ability to fly drones for any non-recreational purpose, which is a genuinely valuable thing to hold.

The entire question of "do I need a license?" collapses into a single decision: **is this flight purely recreational, or is there any other purpose behind it?** Get that answer right and the path picks itself.

The trap — and it's a big one — is that most people badly misjudge what counts as "any other purpose." So before we cover what each path requires, we have to talk about the word that trips up more new pilots than any other.

## The Word "Commercial" Is Broader Than You Think

When most people hear "commercial drone use," they picture a contractor with a fleet, an invoice, a logo on a truck. They assume "recreational" is the default and "commercial" is the rare, obvious exception you'd know if you were in.

It's the reverse. The FAA's definition of recreational is *narrow*, and everything outside it is non-recreational. The test isn't "am I running a drone business?" It's closer to: **is this flight done strictly for personal enjoyment, with no other purpose or benefit?** If the honest answer is anything other than a clean yes, you're very likely in Part 107 territory.

Here's the part that surprises people. All of the following are non-recreational — Part 107 — even though many of them don't *feel* like a business:

- **Getting paid to fly.** The obvious one. The realtor's $300 puts you squarely under Part 107.
- **Flying for your own business.** You own a landscaping company and you fly your drone to photograph a yard you're bidding on. Nobody paid you to fly — but the flight serves your business. That's commercial.
- **A "free" favor for a friend's business.** Your buddy owns a roofing company and you fly his drone up to check a customer's shingles "just to help out." No money changed hands. Still commercial — the flight furthered a business.
- **Real estate photos.** Even if it's your own house you're selling, footage created to help sell property is generally a business purpose.
- **Roof, tower, or property inspections.** Anything where the *output* of the flight informs a business decision or service.
- **Monetized content.** This is the one that catches the coastline-clip crowd. If your YouTube channel runs ads, if your reel is on a monetized account, if the footage is part of building a personal brand you intend to make money from — the FAA's guidance has long treated content created in furtherance of a business as non-recreational. A truly casual post to friends is one thing; building a monetized following is another.

Notice the through-line: **intent and benefit matter, not whether cash physically changed hands.** "Was I paid?" is the wrong question. The right question is "did this flight serve any purpose beyond my own enjoyment?" A flight you'd have flown anyway, for free, just for fun — but which *also* happens to benefit a business — is still non-recreational. The recreational exception is for flights with no purpose but the flying itself.

So, the coastline video: if you posted it to a personal account for friends, purely because it was beautiful and you wanted to share it, that's a defensible recreational flight. The moment that account is monetized, or the moment you fly the *next* coastline shot specifically to grow a following you plan to earn from, or the moment you say yes to the realtor — you've crossed into Part 107.

## The Gray Areas That Trip People Up

Even with the principle clear, a few situations genuinely sit in the fog. Here's how to think about the ones that come up most:

**"I posted it but I'm not making money — yet."** The most common gray zone. A single beautiful clip on a non-monetized personal account, shared because you're proud of it, leans recreational. But the second you turn on monetization, chase brand deals, or fly *in order to* produce content for a channel you're building as income, the purpose of the flight has changed. The flight, not just the upload, is what gets evaluated.

**"It was a one-time favor, no money."** The favor-for-a-business case feels harmless and is one of the most-busted assumptions out there. Flying your neighbor's field so he can show it to a buyer, photographing a friend's food truck for their menu — these further someone's business purpose, and "I didn't charge" doesn't convert them back to recreational.

**"I'm building a portfolio."** Practicing your craft is recreational *if* the flying itself is the point. But shooting footage specifically to assemble a reel you'll use to land paid work is arguably furthering a future business. The honest gut-check: would you fly this exact shot if you knew no one would ever see it and it could never lead to work? If the answer is no, the flight has a non-recreational purpose.

When a situation truly sits in the fog and you can't cleanly call it recreational, **the safe move is to treat it as Part 107.** When in doubt, fly under Part 107 — it covers everything the recreational exception covers and then some. There's no downside to holding the certificate; there's real downside to needing it and not having it.

## What the Recreational Path Actually Requires

If you've honestly landed on "purely for fun," the recreational path is refreshingly light. Here's the whole list:

1. **Pass TRUST.** The Recreational UAS Safety Test — TRUST — is a free, online, multiple-choice test you take through an FAA-approved provider. You can't actually fail it in a way that matters; it walks you through the answers and you finish with a completion certificate. It never expires. Print or save that certificate — you're required to carry proof you've passed and show it to FAA or law enforcement on request.

2. **Register your drone if it weighs 0.55 lb (250 grams) or more.** Registration costs **$5**, is done online, and is valid for **three years**. Most camera drones cross the 250g line; many of the smallest "sub-250" models don't require registration for recreational flying (though they still must follow the rest of the rules). When you register, you get a registration number that must be marked on the aircraft. One nice perk of the recreational path: a single registration covers **all** the drones you fly for fun — you register yourself once, not each aircraft. (That's different on the commercial side, as you'll see below.)

3. **Fly within community-based safety guidelines.** Stay at or below **400 feet AGL**, keep the drone within **visual line of sight** (yours or a visual observer's), give way to and never interfere with manned aircraft, and don't fly carelessly or recklessly.

4. **Get airspace authorization where it's required.** Recreational flyers still have to respect airspace. In controlled airspace (near airports — Class B, C, D, and the surface areas of E) you need authorization through **LAANC** before you fly. In uncontrolled (Class G) airspace you generally don't, but the airspace check is still on you.

5. **Keep it strictly recreational.** This is the condition under everything else — the moment the flight isn't purely for fun, you've stepped off this path entirely and the recreational requirements no longer cover you.

That's genuinely it. An afternoon of effort, $5, and a free test, and a hobbyist is fully legal.

## What the Part 107 Path Actually Requires

The commercial path asks more, because you're being trusted to operate in shared airspace for purposes beyond your own enjoyment. The core requirements:

1. **Be at least 16 years old.** Hard floor.

2. **Pass the Part 107 Aeronautical Knowledge Test.** This is a real, proctored exam taken at an **FAA-approved testing center**, and it costs around **$175**. It covers airspace classifications, reading sectional charts, weather and METAR/TAF interpretation, aircraft loading, crew resource management, and the regulations themselves. It's studyable — most people pass with a few weeks of focused prep — but it is not a walk-through like TRUST. After passing, you apply for your **Remote Pilot Certificate**, which includes a one-time **TSA security background check** as part of the application — routine for most applicants, but build in a little time for it to clear.

3. **Register every drone you fly commercially — regardless of weight.** This is a key difference from the recreational rules. There's no 250g exemption on the commercial side. Even a sub-250g drone must be registered if you're flying it commercially.

4. **Stay current with recurrent training every 24 months.** Your certificate doesn't expire, but to keep exercising its privileges you must complete **free online recurrent training every 24 months**. Miss the window and you're not legally current — you can't fly commercially again until you complete it. This is the requirement pilots most often let slip, because it's free, online, and easy to forget two years out from the day you were thinking about it least.

Once you hold a current Part 107 certificate, you can fly for essentially any purpose — paid work, your own business, monetized content, inspections, mapping, the realtor's listing, all of it — within the operating rules (≤400 ft, visual line of sight, daylight or with the proper night provisions, airspace authorization where required, and so on).

## Why Getting It Wrong Is Expensive

It's tempting to treat this as a "they'll never catch me" situation. Sometimes that's true. But the downside when it isn't is steep.

Flying commercially without a Part 107 certificate is a violation of the federal aviation regulations, and the FAA can pursue **civil penalties measured in thousands of dollars per violation.** The often-cited maximum civil penalty runs up to around **$27,500**, and in the most egregious or reckless cases — especially where safety was endangered — the exposure can climb into criminal territory with much higher penalties. Realistically, a first-time paperwork violation by an otherwise-careful pilot is unlikely to land at the maximum. But "unlikely to be the worst case" is a thin thing to bet a few thousand dollars on, and enforcement does happen — often kicked off by a complaint from a competitor or a property owner.

Set the dollar figures aside, though, because there's a simpler point. The cost of *compliance* is a $175 test and a few weeks of study. The cost of *non-compliance* is an open-ended risk that follows every flight you make. That math isn't close.

## You're Covered Either Way

Here's the reassuring part, and it's why this question is less scary than it first feels: **both paths are legitimate, and you don't have to pick a lane forever.** Plenty of pilots fly recreationally on weekends and hold Part 107 for the occasional paid job. The two aren't in tension — Part 107 simply gives you more room to operate.

What matters is knowing, for each specific flight, which set of rules you're under — and being able to *prove* you met them. That's true whether you're a hobbyist showing a TRUST certificate and a registration number, or a commercial pilot tracking a 24-month recurrent clock and logging flights for clients.

## Back to the DM

So you're staring at the realtor's message, cursor blinking. Here's the clean read on your situation: the coastline clip, posted to a personal account just because it was beautiful, was probably fine as a recreational flight. But the listing shoot she's asking about — paid, for her business, to help sell a property — is unambiguously commercial. Saying yes on your current TRUST-only footing would be flying outside the rules.

That doesn't mean saying no. It means saying *"give me a few weeks."* Study for the Part 107 knowledge test, pass it, get your Remote Pilot Certificate, register your drone on the commercial side, and then take the job — fully legal, with a certificate that opens the door to every paid flight after this one too. The realtor's $300 is the first of many if you want it to be.

The pilots who get tripped up by this aren't reckless. They're enthusiastic — they got good fast, an opportunity showed up, and they said yes before they understood the line they were stepping over. The pilots who do it right pause at exactly the moment you're pausing now, figure out which path the flight belongs to, and get covered before they launch.

You asked the right question at the right time. Now you know the answer.

Plan it. Fly it. Prove it.
