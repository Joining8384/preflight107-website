## The Rule That Quietly Grounds a Lot of Jobs

Here's a Part 107 fact that trips up new commercial pilots: by default, **you are not allowed to fly your drone over people who aren't directly participating in your operation.** Not the crowd at the event, not pedestrians on the sidewalk, not the guests at the wedding.

For years that meant a lot of the most valuable shots — the packed venue, the busy street, the ceremony — were technically off-limits without a waiver. Then in 2021 the FAA's **Operations Over People (OOP)** rule changed the game. It created a path to fly over people legally, *if* your aircraft meets one of four safety categories. Knowing which category your drone falls into is the difference between a legal shoot and a violation.

## Why "Over People" Is Regulated So Hard

The whole point is injury risk. A drone that loses power over an empty field lands in dirt. The same drone over a crowd can hurt someone. So the FAA built the rules around **how much harm the aircraft could do if it fell** — lighter drones with no sharp, exposed spinning parts are treated as lower-risk and given more freedom; heavier ones with more injury potential are restricted.

That's the logic behind the four categories: they're sorted by weight and by how much energy the drone would transfer in a worst-case impact.

## The Four Categories, in Plain English

### Category 1 — the little guys (≤ 0.55 lb / 250g)

If your drone weighs **0.55 pounds (250 grams) or less** and has no exposed rotating parts that could lacerate skin, it qualifies for Category 1. This is the easy category, and it's why the sub-250g class (DJI Mini series and similar) is so popular for this kind of work — many of them can legally fly over people. **Remote ID is required.**

### Category 2 — light, with a manufacturer's blessing

Heavier than 250g, but the manufacturer has demonstrated the drone **won't cause more than a set injury threshold** on impact (technically, under 11 foot-pounds of energy) and has no lacerating parts. These drones get a manufacturer declaration and can fly over people, including sustained flight over them.

### Category 3 — mid-tier, with a big catch

A higher injury threshold (under 25 foot-pounds), still no lacerating parts. Category 3 lets you fly over people **but NOT over open-air assemblies** — meaning **no flying over crowds.** You can only operate over people who are inside a restricted-access area (everyone there knows a drone is flying) or briefly transit over people, not hover above them. This is the category that catches pilots off guard: it sounds permissive until you realize the crowd — the exact thing you wanted to film — is excluded.

### Category 4 — the certified route

Drones with an FAA airworthiness certificate. Rare in the consumer/prosumer world; mostly relevant to specialized operations.

## The Hard Line: Crowds (Open-Air Assemblies)

If you take one thing away, make it this: **flying over an open-air assembly of people — a concert, a parade, a packed stadium, a festival — is only allowed with a Category 1, 2, or 4 aircraft.** Category 3 is specifically excluded from crowds.

So the honest answer to "can I fly over the crowd at this event?" is: **only if you're flying a drone that qualifies for Category 1 or 2** (for most pilots, that means a sub-250g drone or a heavier one with the right manufacturer declaration). Show up to a festival with a big Category 3 aircraft and the crowd shots are off the table, rule-wise.

## Moving Vehicles Get Their Own Version

The rule handles **flying over moving vehicles** with a parallel set of category-based limits. The short version: it's similarly restricted, and open-air-assembly-style rules apply — don't assume that because you can fly over a person you can freely fly over busy traffic. Check the specific limits for your category before you plan a shot over a road.

## Remote ID Is Part of the Deal

Operations over people require your drone to be broadcasting **Remote ID.** If you're planning this kind of work, make sure your aircraft is Remote ID compliant (most current models are, or can be with a broadcast module) — it's not optional for flying over people.

## The Practical Takeaway for Commercial Pilots

If flying over people is core to your work — events, weddings, real estate with people present, busy urban shoots — the cleanest path for most pilots is a **sub-250g Category 1 drone**, which sidesteps most of the complexity. If you fly something heavier, **know your category and its limits cold** before you accept a job that puts people under your aircraft, and never assume "small enough to feel safe" equals "legal over a crowd." When in doubt, keep people out from under the drone, or pursue the proper category/waiver path.

## Where PreFlight 107 Fits

PreFlight 107 keeps your aircraft details in your Hangar and builds them into your **Mission Briefing** — so when you're planning a job that involves people, your drone, its weight class, and the operation are documented in one place, alongside the airspace and weather. It doesn't make the category call for you (that's a rules-and-equipment question), but it keeps the paperwork that proves a professional, deliberate operation ready to go.

## The Bottom Line

Part 107's default is "no flying over non-participants," and the **Operations Over People rule** is the legal way around it — through **four categories sorted by injury risk.** Category 1 (sub-250g) is the simple path; Category 2 needs a manufacturer declaration; **Category 3 can't fly over crowds at all;** Category 4 is the certified route. Flying over an open-air assembly means Category 1, 2, or 4 only, and Remote ID is required throughout. Know your drone's category before you plan a shot over people — it's the line between a legal deliverable and a violation.
