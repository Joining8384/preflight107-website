## The Argument Every Drone Pilot Eventually Has

You're flying legally, minding your own business, and someone comes out yelling that you can't fly over *their* property. It's the single most common confrontation in droning, and it usually comes down to a misunderstanding about who owns the sky.

Here's the short version, then the important nuance: **in general, you can legally fly a drone over private property, because the property owner does not own the airspace above their land.** But — and this matters — "the airspace is legal" doesn't mean "anything goes." Privacy laws, where you take off from, and how you fly can still land you in real trouble. Let's untangle it.

## Who Owns the Sky? (Not the Homeowner)

The foundational idea a lot of angry neighbors get wrong: **you do not own the airspace above your property up to infinity.** That old notion was settled decades ago. A landmark U.S. Supreme Court case established that a property owner controls only the *immediate reaches* of the air above their land — the low space they can actually use and enjoy — not the navigable airspace above it.

The **navigable airspace is a public highway**, and it's regulated by the FAA — a federal agency. That's why a helicopter or a crop duster can fly over your house without knocking on your door, and it's the same principle that lets a drone transit overhead. The FAA, not the landowner, governs who flies where.

So when someone says "you can't fly over my property," the airspace part is, in most cases, simply not true.

## But "Legal Airspace" Isn't a Free Pass

This is where a lot of pilots get overconfident. The FAA governs the *airspace* — it does **not** override every other law that applies to how you behave. Several things can still bite you even when your flight is airspace-legal:

### 1. Where you take off and land

The airspace is public, but the **ground is not.** Standing on someone's lawn to launch, or landing your drone on their property without permission, is old-fashioned **trespassing** — and that has nothing to do with the FAA. Launch and recover from public land or your own property (or land you have permission to use).

### 2. Privacy laws

Flying *over* a property to get somewhere is very different from **hovering outside a bedroom window with a camera.** Many states and cities have drone-specific privacy, voyeurism, and harassment laws, and general privacy and "peeping" statutes can apply too. Using your drone to surveil, record someone in a private setting, or harass a neighbor can be illegal even if the flight itself is in legal airspace. The camera changes the legal picture.

### 3. Reckless operation

Part 107 (and the recreational rules) prohibit **careless or reckless flying.** Buzzing people, flying aggressively low over someone's yard to intimidate them, or creating a hazard is a violation regardless of who owns the land. "I was in legal airspace" is not a defense for flying like a jerk.

## "Can Someone Shoot Down My Drone?" — No.

Because feelings run hot, this comes up a lot. **No, a property owner cannot legally shoot down your drone.** Under federal law, a drone is an *aircraft*, and shooting at any aircraft is a **federal crime** — on top of the obvious dangers of firing a gun into the air and the civil liability for destroying your equipment. Someone who shoots down, nets, or otherwise destroys your drone is the one breaking serious law, not you. (That doesn't make it fun to deal with — see the de-escalation note below.)

## The Smart-Pilot Version of All This

Being *legally* allowed to fly over a property and being *smart* about it aren't the same thing. The pilots who avoid trouble follow a simple code:

- **Don't loiter over homes.** Transit, get your shot, move on. A drone hovering over a backyard reads as surveillance even when it isn't.
- **Keep the camera pointed at your subject**, not into people's windows or fenced yards.
- **Launch and land on ground you're allowed to be on.**
- **De-escalate.** If someone confronts you, you're almost never going to win by quoting regulations at them. Be calm, be brief, and if it's getting heated, it's usually easier to relocate than to be right.
- **Know your state's rules.** Drone privacy laws vary a lot by state — what's fine in one place may be restricted in another.

## Where PreFlight 107 Fits

The property question is really two questions stacked together: *is the airspace legal, and am I flying responsibly?* PreFlight 107 handles the first half — it shows you the **airspace class and any restrictions at your exact launch point** (controlled airspace, TFRs, LAANC ceilings), so you know the airspace itself is clear before you fly. The responsible-flying half — privacy, launch points, courtesy — is on you, but at least you'll never be guessing about the airspace.

## The Bottom Line

Homeowners don't own the sky, and you can generally **fly over private property legally** because the FAA governs the navigable airspace, not the landowner. But that only covers the *airspace* — **trespassing to launch, violating privacy laws, and reckless flying are all still off-limits**, and no one is allowed to shoot your drone down. Fly over, not into people's lives; keep your takeoffs on legal ground; know your state's privacy rules; and when someone's upset, it's usually smarter to move than to argue.
