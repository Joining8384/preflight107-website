## The Knock on the Door

Every drone pilot's quiet fear: you finish a perfectly legal flight, post the footage online, and three weeks later you get an email from the FAA asking for documentation. Or worse — you're at a public park, mid-flight, and someone in a uniform walks up and politely asks for your Part 107 certificate, drone registration, and "the records for this operation."

It happens more often than you'd think. The FAA has been actively investigating drone complaints since Part 107 took effect, and the number of "did you have authorization for this airspace?" letters has grown every year. The good news: pilots who keep proper records almost always come out fine. The bad news: pilots who *can't produce* records are the ones who get fined.

This article walks through exactly what FAA inquiries look like, what they ask for, what you're legally required to produce, and how to prepare so you're never caught flat-footed.

## The Three Kinds of FAA Inquiry

Not all inquiries are equal. Knowing which one you're facing tells you what to expect.

### 1. The Ramp Check
The least intimidating but the most surprising. An FAA inspector — or sometimes a local law enforcement officer acting on a tip — approaches you in the field during or immediately after a flight. They ask for credentials and operational records on the spot. You're not in trouble, they're verifying compliance.

### 2. The Written Inquiry / Letter of Investigation (LOI)
You receive an email or certified letter from the FAA's Flight Standards District Office (FSDO) referencing a specific flight, date, location, or complaint. You're typically given 10–30 days to respond with documentation. This is when paperwork matters most.

### 3. The Civil Action / Enforcement
If the FAA decides a violation occurred, they may pursue a civil penalty (fine) or — in serious cases — suspend your Part 107 certificate. By this point you should have legal counsel. The records you produced in step #2 directly affect the outcome.

## What the FAA Actually Asks For

Here's the standard list of records the FAA can request for any Part 107 operation. Some are required by regulation; others are "voluntary" but expected.

### Legally required (per 14 CFR Part 107)

| Item | What it proves |
|---|---|
| **Remote Pilot Certificate** (current, with photo ID) | You're authorized to fly under Part 107 |
| **FAA Drone Registration** for the aircraft you flew | The drone is legally registered to you |
| **Aircraft Registration Marking** physically on the drone | Required by Part 48 — must be readable without tools |
| **Operating limitations and conditions of your authorization** (LAANC approval, Part 107 waiver, etc.) | You had legal authorization for the airspace at that time |

### Strongly expected (best-practice records)

| Item | What it proves |
|---|---|
| **Pre-flight inspection record** | You verified airworthiness before flight (107.49) |
| **Weather check record** with timestamp | You assessed conditions per 107.51 |
| **Airspace check record** | You verified airspace classification and any restrictions |
| **Flight log** with date, time, location, duration, drone serial, and observer | You logged the operation |
| **Insurance policy** (if commercial operation) | Risk was covered (not required by FAA but often by client contracts) |
| **Photos / video of the operation** | Corroborates the location, altitude, and conditions you reported |

### Often requested for specific scenarios

- **LAANC approval confirmation** (PDF or screenshot) if you flew in controlled airspace
- **Part 107 waiver** if you flew at night, BVLOS, over people, or above 400 ft
- **TFR awareness record** if there was an active TFR near your flight area
- **Visual Observer details** if a VO was used (107.33)
- **Recurrent training record** showing you completed the 24-month recurrent test

## The "Can You Produce This Within 10 Days?" Test

Pull out your phone right now and ask yourself, for your most recent flight:

1. What airspace was I in? Was it controlled or uncontrolled?
2. What was the wind, visibility, and cloud ceiling at exactly that time?
3. What was my LAANC ceiling (if applicable)?
4. Were any TFRs active within 5 nm of my launch site?
5. What's my Part 107 certificate number? When did I last complete recurrent?
6. What's my drone's FAA registration number? Serial number?
7. What's my insurance policy number?

If you couldn't answer any of those in 30 seconds, **you're not ready for an FAA inquiry**. This is exactly the gap that costs pilots money.

## The Old Way — Manual Records

Until recently, the only way to be FAA-ready was a manual paper trail:

- A leather pilot logbook (or Excel spreadsheet) for flight records
- Screenshots of UAV Forecast or NWS weather pages saved to Google Drive folders
- PDF prints of LAANC approval emails, stapled to the paper logbook
- Photos of the drone before each flight, organized in dated folders
- A separate document with your Part 107 cert, FAA reg, and insurance PDFs

This works, but it has a fatal flaw: when the FAA asks, **you have to manually assemble all of it.** That assembly process can take 30+ minutes per flight if you're working from memory, photos, and emails. Multiply by ten flights and you're looking at half a day of paperwork archeology — for a single inquiry.

The other problem: any of those records can be questioned for authenticity. Was that weather screenshot from the actual flight time? Did you really check airspace before launching, or after-the-fact?

## What If the FAA Already Reached Out and You Don't Have Records?

It happens. Some advice:

1. **Don't lie or fabricate records.** This turns a small problem into a much bigger one.
2. **Be honest about what you have and don't have.** "I didn't keep a structured log of weather conditions, but here are the screenshots from my phone at the approximate time" is workable. "I had all the records but my computer crashed" is not.
3. **Cooperate fully with the inquiry.** The FAA's process generally rewards good-faith cooperation. Pilots who get defensive or evasive tend to get harsher outcomes.
4. **Consider hiring an aviation attorney** if the inquiry escalates beyond a basic request. AOPA's Pilot Legal Services Plan (~$45/year) is a worthwhile insurance policy.
5. **Start logging properly from this point forward.** Even if the current inquiry doesn't go well, future flights will be airtight.

## The Bottom Line

The FAA isn't out to get drone pilots. Most inspectors are just verifying that you're doing the job the rules require. But the rules require *proof*, not memory — and "I'm pretty sure I checked the weather" doesn't hold up next to "here's a timestamped PDF showing the METAR, airspace, TFRs, and pilot certification for that exact flight."

The pilots who get fined are almost never the ones who *broke* the rules accidentally. They're the ones who can't *prove* they followed them.

Whether you use PreFlight 107's Auto-Evidence feature or build your own paper trail, the principle is the same: **make every flight reproducible on demand.** Future-you will thank present-you.

Fly safe out there.
