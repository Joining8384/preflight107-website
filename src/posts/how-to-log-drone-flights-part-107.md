## Does the FAA Require Flight Logs?

The short answer: not explicitly for every flight. FAA Part 107 doesn't mandate a specific logbook format the way manned aviation regulations do. But several Part 107 rules *implicitly* require records — and in practice, flying without logs is a liability you don't want.

Here's why. Part 107.12 requires remote pilots to make their certificate available for inspection by an FAA representative or law enforcement. If you're ever questioned after an incident, a timestamped flight log is the difference between "I was compliant" and "I can't prove it."

Beyond incidents, logs matter for:

- **Maintenance tracking** — Part 107.49 requires you to keep the sUAS in a condition for safe operation. Logging flight hours helps you track when to inspect motors, props, and batteries.
- **Insurance claims** — insurers routinely ask for flight records when evaluating claims.
- **Client documentation** — commercial clients increasingly expect proof-of-flight records for project files and liability coverage.
- **Waiver compliance** — if you operate under a Part 107 waiver (night flights, over people, etc.), the FAA may require you to submit records as part of that authorization.

The FAA doesn't audit recreational flyers the same way — but if you hold a Part 107 certificate and fly commercially, building a log habit from day one is the professional standard.

## What to Include in Every Flight Log

No single required format exists, but a complete log entry should cover the following fields:

- **Date and local time** of the flight
- **Location** — GPS coordinates or a clear description of the site
- **Duration** — wheels-up to wheels-down
- **Aircraft** — make, model, and serial number (or your internal drone ID)
- **Pilot in command** — your name and certificate number
- **Flight purpose** — commercial job, training, inspection, etc.
- **Weather conditions** — wind speed, visibility, sky condition at time of flight
- **Airspace class** and any LAANC authorization number if applicable
- **Incidents or anomalies** — even minor ones worth noting
- **Battery cycles** — logged per battery is even better

That last one surprises new pilots. LiPo batteries degrade with each charge cycle and are a leading cause of in-flight failures. Logging cycles per battery lets you retire them before they become a safety risk, not after.

## Paper vs. Digital Logs

Paper notebooks are legal but create friction — they're hard to search, easy to lose, and don't capture GPS data. A spreadsheet is a step up but still manual.

Digital flight logs tied to your drone's GPS track are the most defensible record you can have. Timestamps pulled from the aircraft's telemetry are harder to dispute than a handwritten entry. If your drone controller or app logs telemetry automatically, export and archive those files alongside your manual entries.

**PreFlight 107** lets you log flights directly in the app — location, aircraft, duration, weather conditions, and notes — with everything synced to your account so it's accessible from your phone or the web dashboard. Pro subscribers can export logs as PDF reports, which is useful for client deliverables and insurance documentation.

## Building the Habit

The biggest challenge with flight logging isn't knowing what to record — it's actually doing it consistently. A few practices that help:

- **Log before you pack up.** Don't wait until you're back at the office. Fill in the entry while the details are fresh, ideally before you put the aircraft back in its case.
- **Use a checklist.** A preflight checklist that ends with "open log" makes logging part of the mission cadence, not an afterthought.
- **Batch nothing.** Reconstructing three flights from memory at the end of the week produces inaccurate records. One entry per flight, every flight.
- **Log the anomalies.** If a motor sounded different, a battery discharged faster than expected, or GPS acquisition took longer than usual — write it down. These are the early warnings that prevent incidents.

## What Happens If You Don't Log?

For most routine flights, nothing immediately. The FAA isn't monitoring your logbook in real time. But the risk is asymmetric: the cost of logging is a few minutes per flight, and the cost of *not* logging is potential liability exposure, voided insurance, and no paper trail to defend yourself if something goes wrong.

A single incident without documentation — a flyaway, a near-miss, property damage — can result in civil liability and FAA enforcement action. A log doesn't make the incident disappear, but it demonstrates the professionalism and diligence that regulators and courts take into account.

## The Bottom Line

Treat your flight log the way a manned pilot treats their logbook: as a professional record of your time in the air. The FAA may not always be watching, but your future self — the one dealing with an insurance claim, an irate client, or an FAA inquiry — will be glad you kept one.

Log every flight. Log it right. And keep those records somewhere they won't disappear.

*Fly safe out there.*
